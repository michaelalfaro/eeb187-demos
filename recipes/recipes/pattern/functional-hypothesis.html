Recipe 16: Functional Hypothesis — Conspicuousness vs. Defense

Are the most conspicuous species in my family also the most defended?

The Big Question

Are the most conspicuous species in my family also the most defended — and does that relationship hold after controlling for shared evolutionary history?

This Is an Instance Of…

Testing a Functional Hypothesis about Color / Aposematism

You are asking whether a predicted functional pairing — conspicuous color signals danger — holds at the macroevolutionary scale. This is an aposematism hypothesis: species that are venomous, spinous, or otherwise dangerous should benefit from advertising that fact with high-contrast, easily detected color patterns.

The method is a phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS) regression of a continuous color metric (mean chromatic boundary strength, m_dS) against a binary ecological trait (defended vs. harmless). Because related species share body plans and life histories, a standard t-test would inflate your false-positive rate. PGLS corrects for this by modeling the covariance among species as a Brownian motion process on the phylogeny.

This recipe tests one of the central predictions of honest signaling theory at a scale — an entire reef fish family — where most behavioral experiments cannot go.

Why This Matters for Your Clade

Natural fits for this recipe

Balistoidea (Triggerfishes and filefishes) — Triggerfishes have an erectile dorsal spine locked in place by a second “trigger” spine; some species are also toxic. The family spans spectacularly bold patterns (Balistoides conspicillum, Rhinecanthus aculeatus) and cryptic plains (Aluterus monoceros). If conspicuousness tracks defense, you should see higher m_dS in the venomous or traumatogenic species.

Acanthuridae (Surgeonfishes) — All surgeonfishes carry caudal scalpels — sharp bony plates at the base of the tail — which qualifies them as traumatogenic under FishBase coding. But scalpel sharpness varies widely. This clade lets you ask whether the species that are also brightly colored are sending an honest warning about their scalpels, or whether color serves a different function entirely.

Other groups — Even if your clade has few defended species, running this recipe will tell you something: if there is no defended / conspicuous correlation, that is itself an interesting result. It suggests that color in your group is driven by something other than defense advertising.

Aposematism is well-documented in invertebrates and amphibians, but reef fish aposematism is genuinely understudied. A PGLS result in your family, even a null result, is a meaningful contribution.

The Method

Step 1 — Pull defense status from FishBase. FishBase codes each species with a Dangerous field. Values include: harmless, venomous, traumatogenic, poisonous to eat, other (see remarks). Use pull_fishbase() from helpers.R to retrieve this field for your species list.

Step 2 — Collapse to a binary variable. The detailed FishBase categories are not balanced enough for a regression with a small dataset. Collapse to two categories: "defended" (venomous, traumatogenic, or poisonous to eat) vs. "harmless". Drop species coded as "other (see remarks)" or NA.

Step 3 — Merge with your pavo metrics. Join the defense binary to your pavo CSV by species name. The column you will use as the response is m_dS (mean chromatic boundary strength — the strongest single measure of how visually conspicuous color boundaries are).

Step 4 — Match to tree. Use match_tree_data() to prune tree and data to their shared species. Check that you have at least 5 species in each category — if one category has fewer than 5, PGLS will run but the result will be unreliable.

Step 5 — Run PGLS. Use safe_pgls() which wraps nlme::gls() with a Brownian motion correlation structure. Your formula is m_dS ~ defended.

Step 6 — Visualize. A boxplot with individual points overlaid, colored by defense status, is your main figure. Make it with ggplot2 + theme_poster.

How to Interpret Results

PGLS result Interpretation
p < 0.05, defended > harmless Defended species have stronger chromatic contrast; consistent with aposematism
p < 0.05, defended < harmless Defended species are less conspicuous; inconsistent with aposematism (consider crypsis + chemical defense)
p > 0.05 No detectable conspicuousness-defense association after phylogenetic correction
Coefficient near 0 The effect size is small regardless of p-value — report both

Pitfalls to avoid:

  • Conspicuous ≠ aposematic. A species can be conspicuous for mating signals, species recognition, or other reasons. Aposematism requires both conspicuousness and defense. A significant result is consistent with aposematism, not proof of it.
  • FishBase defense codes are conservative. “Traumatogenic” covers species with spines that can wound a handler. That does not mean every spine user is sending an honest warning to predators. Mention this limitation in your poster.
  • Sample size asymmetry. If 90% of your species are “harmless,” the PGLS will have low power to detect an effect. Report sample sizes in each group.
  • Ignoring phylogenetic signal in the predictor. If defended species cluster in one part of the tree, the PGLS is doing important work. Check this with a quick tip-coloring plot before running the regression.
  • Using raw gls() without a correlation structure. Always use safe_pgls() or specify corBrownian explicitly. A plain ANOVA or t-test on these data is not appropriate.

The Code

library(ape)
library(ggplot2)

source("https://michaelalfaro.github.io/eeb187-demos/recipes/code/helpers.R")

# ── Load your pavo metrics and tree ──────────────────────────────
bundle <- load_bundle("path/to/your/bundle")
tree   <- bundle$tree
traits <- read.csv("my_pavo_metrics.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
# Required columns: species, m_dS (and others you may want)

# ── Pull defense status from FishBase ────────────────────────────
fb <- pull_fishbase(traits$species,
                    fields = list(species = c("Dangerous")))

cat("FishBase Dangerous values found:\n")
print(table(fb$Dangerous, useNA = "always"))

# ── Merge and recode to binary ────────────────────────────────────
merged <- merge(traits, fb, by = "species")

# Collapse FishBase categories to defended / harmless
# Adjust the right-hand vector if your clade has different categories
merged$defended <- ifelse(
  merged$Dangerous %in% c("venomous", "traumatogenic", "poisonous to eat"),
  "defended", "harmless")

# Drop rows with ambiguous or missing codes
merged <- merged[merged$Dangerous != "other (see remarks)" &
                 !is.na(merged$Dangerous), ]

cat("\nDefense category counts after recoding:\n")
print(table(merged$defended))

# ── Match tree and data ───────────────────────────────────────────
matched <- match_tree_data(tree, merged, species_col = "species")
df   <- matched$data
tree_p <- matched$tree

cat("\nSpecies in final analysis:", nrow(df), "\n")

# ── Check: are defended species clustered on tree? ────────────────
def_colors <- c("harmless" = "#264653", "defended" = "#e76f51")

png("fig-defense-on-tree.png", width = 2200, height = 1800, res = 300)
plot(tree_p, type = "fan", cex = 0.45, edge.width = 2,
     tip.color = def_colors[df$defended[match(tree_p$tip.label, df$species)]])
legend("topleft",
       legend = c("Harmless", "Defended"),
       fill   = c("#264653", "#e76f51"),
       bty    = "n", cex = 1.1)
title("Defense status on phylogeny")
dev.off()

# ── Run PGLS ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
model <- safe_pgls(m_dS ~ defended, df = df, tree = tree_p)
# safe_pgls() prints coefficient, t-value, and p-value automatically

# ── Visualization: boxplot with individual points ─────────────────
p <- ggplot(df, aes(x = defended, y = m_dS, fill = defended)) +
  geom_boxplot(alpha = 0.7, outlier.shape = NA, width = 0.45) +
  geom_jitter(width = 0.15, size = 3, alpha = 0.6, color = "gray20") +
  scale_fill_manual(values = def_colors) +
  labs(title    = "Chromatic contrast by defense status",
       subtitle = "Are defended species more conspicuous? (PGLS-corrected)",
       x        = "",
       y        = "Mean chromatic boundary strength (m_dS)") +
  theme_poster +
  theme(legend.position = "none")

ggsave("fig-defense-conspicuousness.png",
       plot   = p,
       width  = 8,
       height = 7,
       dpi    = 300,
       bg     = "white")

print(p)

# ── Effect size check ─────────────────────────────────────────────
cat("\nGroup means:\n")
print(tapply(df$m_dS, df$defended, mean, na.rm = TRUE))

cat("\nGroup medians:\n")
print(tapply(df$m_dS, df$defended, median, na.rm = TRUE))

What to report on your poster:

  • The boxplot (main figure)
  • PGLS coefficient, t-value, and p-value
  • Group means and sample sizes
  • A plain-language interpretation: “Defended species in [family] show [higher / lower / similar] chromatic boundary strength (PGLS: β = , p = ), [supporting / not supporting] the prediction that conspicuousness tracks defense in this group.”

From the Literature

Cott, H. B. (1940). Adaptive Coloration in Animals. Methuen, London. Chapters 8–9. The foundational natural history account of warning coloration across animals. Cott coins much of the terminology still in use (aposematism, flash coloration, disruptive coloration) and documents reef fish examples alongside amphibians and insects. Required reading before making any aposematism argument.

Maan, M. E., & Cummings, M. E. (2012). Poison frog colors are honest signals of toxicity, particularly for bird predators. American Naturalist, 179, E1–E14. A clean test of the aposematism prediction — conspicuousness correlates with toxicity across poison frog species — using phylogenetic comparative methods. The logical structure of their analysis (color metric ~ defense level, PGLS) is directly parallel to this recipe.

Kikuchi, D. W., & Pfennig, D. W. (2013). Imperfect mimicry and the limits of natural selection. Quarterly Review of Biology, 88, 297–315. Reviews the conditions under which warning coloration is and is not expected to evolve: receiver psychology, predator learning, and prey density all matter. Useful for framing why a null result in your PGLS does not disprove aposematism — it may instead reflect that fish predators respond differently than bird predators, or that your clade’s defenses are cryptic rather than advertised.
