Recipe 17: Stripe Orientation and Ecology

Do vertical bars vs. horizontal stripes predict where a fish lives?

The Big Question

Do vertical versus horizontal stripe patterns correlate with habitat or behavior — and does that relationship hold after controlling for phylogeny?

This Is an Instance Of…

Testing a Pattern-Function Hypothesis

You are testing whether stripe orientation — one of the most salient and ecologically interpreted features of reef fish color — predicts where a species lives or how it behaves. The pavo metric A (aspect ratio of color patches) is your quantitative proxy: values above 1 indicate patches taller than wide (vertical bars); values below 1 indicate patches wider than tall (horizontal stripes).

The ecological predictor can be categorical (FishBase DemersPelag habitat codes: reef-associated, demersal, pelagic, etc.) or continuous (FishBase DepthRangeDeep, the maximum recorded depth). The analysis is a PGLS regression that asks whether habitat type predicts stripe orientation after accounting for shared ancestry.

This recipe connects visual ecology theory — the prediction that vertical bars disrupt outline against vertical reef structure while horizontal stripes aid schooling in open water — to a macroevolutionary test across your entire clade.

Why This Matters for Your Clade

The orientation of stripes is not arbitrary. Behavioral and visual ecology studies suggest two main functional predictions:

  • Vertical bars create disruptive coloration against the complex vertical geometry of coral reefs (coral branches, sea fans, crevices). They also disrupt the perception of body outline for edge-detecting predators.
  • Horizontal stripes facilitate schooling: a horizontal stripe makes it easier for conspecifics to track each other’s movements, and in open-water schools they contribute to the “dazzle” effect that confuses predators trying to track a single fish.
Natural fits for this recipe

Acanthuridae (Surgeonfishes) — The genus Naso is largely pelagic and plain or horizontally striped; the reef-associated surgeonfishes (Acanthurus, Ctenochaetus, Zebrasoma) are more variable. Does habitat type predict stripe orientation across the family?

Pomacanthidae (Angelfishes) — Many angelfishes have dramatic vertical banding. Are the most vertically banded species also the most tightly reef-associated (shallower, less pelagic)?

Halichoerine wrasses — This large clade spans habitats from exposed reef crests to rubble zones and even deeper water. With 50–70 species, you have the sample size to detect a depth–orientation signal.

Balistoidea — Triggerfishes that form midwater schools (Melichthys) show more horizontal color elements than the solitary reef-dwelling species. Does DemersPelag or depth predict this in the broader family?

Any clade — Even if the prediction does not pan out, the absence of a habitat–orientation correlation is ecologically informative. It might mean that stripe orientation in your group is primarily driven by species recognition or sexual selection rather than habitat-mediated crypsis.

The Method

Step 1 — Identify your predictor. Choose one of two options: - Categorical: FishBase DemersPelag codes (reef-associated, demersal, benthopelagic, pelagic). Collapse to two groups if needed (reef-associated vs. not-reef-associated), since small sample sizes in each category will limit your analysis. - Continuous: FishBase DepthRangeDeep (maximum depth recorded for the species). Log-transform before analysis.

If you are not sure which to use, start with DepthRangeDeep — it tends to have fewer missing values and gives a continuous gradient to work with.

Step 2 — Pull the predictor from FishBase. Use pull_fishbase() with fields = list(species = c("DemersPelag", "DepthRangeDeep")).

Step 3 — Check your response variable. Your response is A (aspect ratio) from your pavo metrics. Print a histogram. Values near 1 are square patches; values above 1.5 are meaningfully vertically elongated; values below 0.7 are meaningfully horizontal. If most of your species cluster near 1, the signal may be weak regardless of the predictor.

Step 4 — Match to tree and run PGLS. The formula is A ~ DemersPelag (categorical) or log(A) ~ log(DepthRangeDeep) (continuous). Note: if you use log(A), values greater than 0 are vertical (A > 1) and values less than 0 are horizontal (A < 1). This symmetric scale can make interpretation easier.

Step 5 — Visualize. For the categorical predictor: a boxplot of A by habitat category, colored by the two groups. For the continuous predictor: a scatter plot of A versus depth with a PGLS regression line overlaid.

How to Interpret Results

Result Interpretation
Reef-associated species have higher A Consistent with disruptive coloration hypothesis: reef habitat selects for vertical bars
Open-water / pelagic species have lower A Consistent with schooling / dazzle hypothesis: open water selects for horizontal stripes
A increases with maximum depth Deeper species are more vertically barred — could reflect different reef light environments at depth
A decreases with maximum depth Deeper species are more horizontally striped — consider that deeper = more open, less structured reef
No significant relationship Stripe orientation in this clade is not predicted by habitat type; other forces (species recognition, sexual selection) may dominate

Pitfalls to avoid:

  • A near 1 for most species. If your pavo metrics show A clustering around 1 with low variance, the signal will be weak regardless of method. Report the range and variance of A in your sample before interpreting any regression.
  • Reef-associated is not the same as “vertical bar habitat.” Many reef fish hide in sand or rubble, where vertical bars would not help. Refine your habitat categories if FishBase allows it (some species have DepthRange comments that clarify habitat microstructure).
  • Do not log-transform DepthRangeDeep if many species have 0 or very shallow records. Add 1 before logging (log(DepthRangeDeep + 1)) and note this in your methods.
  • Sexual dichromatism confounds the analysis. If you mixed male and female images, and males and females have different orientations (as in many wrasses and parrotfishes), your A values will be noisy. Use the same sex across all species or explicitly note which phase/sex you measured.
  • Small samples in pelagic or open-water categories. Most reef fish families have few true pelagic members. A categorical test with n < 5 in one category will have low power. If that is your situation, switch to the continuous depth predictor.

The Code

library(ape)
library(ggplot2)

source("https://michaelalfaro.github.io/eeb187-demos/recipes/code/helpers.R")

# ── Load your pavo metrics and tree ──────────────────────────────
bundle <- load_bundle("path/to/your/bundle")
tree   <- bundle$tree
traits <- read.csv("my_pavo_metrics.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
# Required columns: species, A (aspect ratio from pavo)

# ── Pull habitat data from FishBase ──────────────────────────────
fb <- pull_fishbase(traits$species,
                    fields = list(species = c("DemersPelag", "DepthRangeDeep")))

cat("DemersPelag categories found:\n")
print(table(fb$DemersPelag, useNA = "always"))

cat("\nDepthRangeDeep summary:\n")
print(summary(fb$DepthRangeDeep))

# ── Merge pavo metrics with FishBase data ─────────────────────────
merged <- merge(traits, fb, by = "species")

# ── Check the distribution of A ───────────────────────────────────
hist(merged$A, breaks = 20,
     main = "Distribution of aspect ratio (A)",
     xlab = "A (> 1 = vertical bars, < 1 = horizontal stripes)",
     col  = "#2a9d8f")
abline(v = 1, col = "#e76f51", lwd = 2, lty = 2)

# ── Option A: Categorical predictor (DemersPelag) ─────────────────
# Collapse to two groups; adjust categories for your clade
merged$habitat <- ifelse(
  merged$DemersPelag %in% c("reef-associated"),
  "reef", "other")
merged_cat <- merged[!is.na(merged$habitat) & !is.na(merged$A), ]

cat("\nHabitat category counts:\n")
print(table(merged_cat$habitat))

matched_cat <- match_tree_data(tree, merged_cat, species_col = "species")

# PGLS: A ~ habitat (categorical)
model_cat <- safe_pgls(A ~ habitat, df = matched_cat$data, tree = matched_cat$tree)

# Boxplot: A by habitat category
p_cat <- ggplot(matched_cat$data, aes(x = habitat, y = A, fill = habitat)) +
  geom_boxplot(alpha = 0.7, outlier.shape = NA, width = 0.45) +
  geom_jitter(width = 0.15, size = 3, alpha = 0.6, color = "gray20") +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 1, linetype = "dashed",
             color = "#e76f51", linewidth = 0.8) +
  annotate("text", x = 0.6, y = 1.05, label = "A = 1\n(square patches)",
           size = 4, color = "#e76f51", hjust = 0) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("reef" = "#2a9d8f", "other" = "#264653")) +
  labs(title    = "Stripe orientation by habitat",
       subtitle = "Reef-associated vs. other habitat (PGLS-corrected)",
       x        = "",
       y        = "Aspect ratio A\n(↑ vertical bars  |  ↓ horizontal stripes)") +
  theme_poster +
  theme(legend.position = "none")

ggsave("fig-stripe-habitat.png",
       plot  = p_cat, width = 8, height = 7, dpi = 300, bg = "white")
print(p_cat)

# ── Option B: Continuous predictor (DepthRangeDeep) ───────────────
merged_dep <- merged[!is.na(merged$DepthRangeDeep) & merged$DepthRangeDeep > 0
                     & !is.na(merged$A), ]
merged_dep$log_depth <- log(merged_dep$DepthRangeDeep)

matched_dep <- match_tree_data(tree, merged_dep, species_col = "species")

# PGLS: A ~ log(depth)
model_dep <- safe_pgls(A ~ log_depth, df = matched_dep$data, tree = matched_dep$tree)

# Scatter plot: A vs. log(max depth)
p_dep <- ggplot(matched_dep$data, aes(x = log_depth, y = A)) +
  geom_point(size = 3.5, alpha = 0.7, color = "#2a9d8f") +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", se = TRUE,
              color = "#e76f51", fill = "#e76f51", alpha = 0.15,
              linewidth = 1.2) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 1, linetype = "dashed",
             color = "gray50", linewidth = 0.7) +
  labs(title    = "Stripe orientation vs. maximum depth",
       subtitle = "Does depth predict vertical vs. horizontal patterning? (PGLS-corrected)",
       x        = "log(Maximum depth, m)",
       y        = "Aspect ratio A\n(↑ vertical bars  |  ↓ horizontal stripes)") +
  theme_poster

ggsave("fig-stripe-depth.png",
       plot  = p_dep, width = 9, height = 7, dpi = 300, bg = "white")
print(p_dep)

What to report on your poster:

  • Your chosen visualization (boxplot for categorical, scatter for continuous)
  • PGLS coefficient, t-value, and p-value
  • Mean A by group (if categorical), or the slope and direction (if continuous)

From the Literature

Stevens, M., & Merilaita, S. (2009). Animal camouflage: current issues and new perspectives. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 364, 423–427. A brief review that frames the main hypotheses for stripe function — background matching, disruptive coloration, and motion dazzle — in a comparative context. Useful for framing your prediction in the Introduction section of the poster.

How, M. J., & Zanker, J. M. (2014). Motion camouflage induced by zebra stripes. Zoology, 117, 163–170. Tests the motion dazzle hypothesis directly: stripe orientation affects how hard it is for a predator to track a moving target. The logic extends to fish: horizontal stripes in schooling fish may make it harder for predators to lock onto individual targets moving horizontally through the water.

Marshall, N. J. (2000). Communication and camouflage with the same “bright” colours in reef fishes. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 355, 1243–1248. A classic paper showing that conspicuous colors in reef fish can serve simultaneously as camouflage (broken outline against reef background) and conspecific communication. Directly relevant to the interpretation of vertical bars: what looks conspicuous to us may be disruptive against coral.
