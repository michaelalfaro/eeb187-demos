Recipe 13: Sexual System and Color Complexity

Do sex-changing species have more complex color patterns than species with fixed sexes?

The Big Question

Do protogynous (female-to-male sex-changing) species have more complex color patterns than gonochoristic (fixed-sex) species, consistent with stronger sexual selection on terminal-phase males?

This Is an Instance Of…

Categorical PGLS — testing whether a discrete life history state predicts a continuous color metric

You are asking whether membership in a sexual-system category predicts color pattern complexity after accounting for phylogeny. This is a phylogenetic ANOVA (implemented as PGLS with a categorical predictor). The logic is: if sexual selection drives color complexity, then species in which males compete intensely for females — especially protogynous species where terminal-phase males achieve dominance after a life spent as female — should show higher pattern complexity than species where sex is fixed from birth and male-male competition is less extreme.

Why This Matters for Your Clade

Protogyny is not randomly distributed across reef fishes. It is concentrated in groups where male body size or territory quality strongly determines reproductive success, exactly the conditions predicted by the size-advantage model of sex change. These are also groups with striking sexual dichromatism — initial-phase versus terminal-phase color differences that can be among the most dramatic on the reef.

Measuring pattern complexity (m, transition density) rather than just hue or brightness lets you ask a more nuanced question: is it just that terminal-phase males are brighter, or are they also more intricately patterned? Theory predicts both — sexual selection should favor conspicuous, complex signals that are hard for females to miss and hard for rival males to fake.

Clade-specific tips
  • Scarinae (parrotfishes): The IP/TP distinction is extreme — initial-phase fish can be female, primary male, or juveniles all sharing the same drab pattern; terminal-phase males are often brilliant blue-green or red. If your images are IP only or TP only, note this clearly and explain what your PGLS result actually tests.
  • Cirrhilabrini (fairy wrasses): Cirrhilabrus males in nuptial display coloration are among the most extravagantly patterned fish on the reef. The genus is largely protogynous. Expect this recipe to be highly relevant to your clade story.
  • Halichoerinae (hogfishes and kin): Some species have dramatic TP males (Lachnolaimus, Bodianus) while others are less dimorphic. Variation within this subfamily makes the between-species contrast especially interesting.
  • Hypsigenyinae (hogfishes, dragon wrasses): Includes species with extreme ontogenetic change (Novaculichthys) and others that are more subtly dichromatic. Mixed sexual systems within the subfamily make this a natural test case.
  • Pomacanthidae (angelfishes): Most angelfishes are gonochoristic, but a few genera (Centropyge, Genicanthus) include protogynous species. If your sample spans both, you can run this recipe directly.
  • Balistoidea (triggerfishes + filefishes): Most are gonochoristic. Expect the “protogynous” category to be very small or absent. If you have fewer than 5 species in one category, report the sample size prominently and interpret with caution.

The Method

You will pull the reproduction table from FishBase for your species, extract the ReproMode column describing the sexual system, and collapse it into a binary predictor: protogynous versus gonochoristic. Species coded as protandrous, simultaneous hermaphrodites, or with missing data are dropped for this analysis (you can note how many were excluded and why).

ReproMode, not Mode

In rfishbase ≤ 3.x the column was called Mode. From rfishbase 5.x onward it was renamed ReproMode. The recipe below uses the 5.x name.

You then run a PGLS with m (transition density, your measure of pattern complexity) as the response and sexual_system as the categorical predictor. PGLS accounts for the fact that related species are not statistically independent — without this correction, a single protogynous clade with high-m species could appear to “explain” variance that is really just phylogenetic clustering.

The boxplot visualizes the distribution of m values in each sexual-system category with individual points overlaid so reviewers can see your raw sample sizes directly.

How to Interpret Results

Result Interpretation What to say on your poster
Protogynous m > gonochoristic m, p < 0.05 Sex-changing species have significantly more complex patterns “Pattern complexity is significantly higher in protogynous species, consistent with stronger sexual selection on terminal-phase males.”
No significant difference, p > 0.05 Sexual system does not predict pattern complexity in this clade “We find no significant relationship between sexual system and pattern complexity, suggesting other factors (habitat, diet, crypsis) may dominate color evolution in our clade.”
Protogynous m < gonochoristic m Unexpected — gonochoristic species are more complex “Gonochoristic species show higher pattern complexity; this could reflect intense male-male competition in species with fixed sex, or it may reflect sampling bias if our protogynous sample is dominated by initial-phase images.”
Very small sample in one category (< 5) Underpowered — result is suggestive only “With only N = [X] protogynous species, statistical power is limited. Results are preliminary and should be interpreted cautiously.”
Common pitfalls
  • Image phase matters enormously. If your pavo metrics were computed from initial-phase images (drab, female-like coloration), you are measuring female pattern complexity, not the male display that sexual selection acts on. Check your image sources and describe clearly which phase your metrics represent.
  • FishBase ReproMode codes are not always clean. The column uses varied free-text entries. The cleaning regex in the code below catches the most common variants, but print table(merged$ReproMode) before cleaning and check for unusual entries (e.g., “hermaphrodite” without direction, “bidirectional sex change”).
  • Dropping species changes your phylogenetic context. When you filter to only protogynous + gonochoristic species, match_tree_data() will prune the tree accordingly. The pruned tree is what PGLS uses — this is correct, but note that your reported N after filtering may be substantially smaller than your total pavo dataset.
  • PGLS with small N is sensitive to outliers. Plot the residuals. If one or two species are driving the result, your interpretation should flag this.

The Code

# ── Recipe 13: Sexual System and Color Complexity ────────────────
source("https://michaelalfaro.github.io/eeb187-demos/recipes/code/helpers.R")   # loads ape, phytools, nlme, ggplot2, theme_poster

# ── 1. Load your pavo metrics and tree ──────────────────────────
traits <- read.csv("path/to/your-pavo-metrics.csv",
                   stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
tree   <- ape::read.tree("path/to/your-family-mini-tree.tre")

# ── 2. Pull sexual system data from FishBase ─────────────────────
# rfishbase 5.x uses `ReproMode` (older versions used `Mode`).
fb <- pull_fishbase(traits$species,
                    fields = list(reproduction = c("ReproMode")))

cat("Raw ReproMode values from FishBase:\n")
print(table(fb$ReproMode, useNA = "always"))

# ── 3. Merge and clean to binary predictor ──────────────────────
merged <- merge(traits, fb, by = "species")

merged$sexual_system <- ifelse(
  grepl("protogyn", merged$ReproMode, ignore.case = TRUE), "protogynous",
  ifelse(grepl("dioec|gonochor", merged$ReproMode, ignore.case = TRUE),
         "gonochoristic", NA))

cat("\nSpecies retained by sexual system:\n")
print(table(merged$sexual_system, useNA = "always"))

# Drop species with NA sexual system (protandrous, simultaneous, missing)
n_before <- nrow(merged)
merged   <- merged[!is.na(merged$sexual_system), ]
cat("\nDropped", n_before - nrow(merged), "species with NA or other sexual system\n")
cat("Final N:", nrow(merged), "\n")

# ── 4. Match tree to filtered data ──────────────────────────────
matched <- match_tree_data(tree, merged, species_col = "species")
cat("Species in PGLS:", nrow(matched$data), "\n")
cat("  Protogynous:", sum(matched$data$sexual_system == "protogynous"), "\n")
cat("  Gonochoristic:", sum(matched$data$sexual_system == "gonochoristic"), "\n")

# ── 5. PGLS: pattern complexity ~ sexual system ──────────────────
model <- safe_pgls(m ~ sexual_system, df = matched$data, tree = matched$tree)
print(summary(model))

# ── 6. Boxplot ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
ggplot(matched$data,
       aes(x = sexual_system, y = m, fill = sexual_system)) +
  geom_boxplot(alpha = 0.7, outlier.shape = NA) +
  geom_jitter(width = 0.15, size = 3, alpha = 0.6, color = "grey30") +
  labs(
    title = "Pattern complexity by sexual system",
    x     = "",
    y     = "Transition density (m)"
  ) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("gonochoristic" = "#264653",
                                "protogynous"   = "#e9c46a")) +
  theme_poster +
  theme(legend.position = "none")

ggsave("results/recipe13-sexual-system-boxplot.pdf",
       width = 6, height = 5)
cat("Saved: results/recipe13-sexual-system-boxplot.pdf\n")

# ── 7. Optional: repeat for other pavo variables ─────────────────
# You can swap m for A, Jc, Jt, m_dS, or m_dL depending on
# whether you have a specific hypothesis about hue vs. pattern.
# e.g.: safe_pgls(Jc ~ sexual_system, df = matched$data, tree = matched$tree)
A note for students using terminal-phase images only

If your image set is drawn primarily from terminal-phase (TP) male photographs — which is common in museum and online databases for wrasses and parrotfishes — then your m values already describe the complex, sexually-selected phenotype. In that case, this recipe tests whether the degree of TP complexity differs between species that achieve that TP status via sex change versus species where males are male from birth. That is still a scientifically interesting question, but it is subtly different from comparing sexes within a species. State clearly in your poster which phenotype your images represent.

From the Literature

Hodge, J. R., Santini, F., & Wainwright, P. C. (2020) — Phylogenetic comparative analysis across 1,187 labrid species showing that colour dimorphism is most pronounced in coral-reef species, and that habitat-specific factors (water clarity, structural refuge) rather than sexual selection per se best explain the pattern. A key reference for any labrid group asking why sex-changers are so colorful. Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 287(1923), 20200167. DOI: 10.1098/rspb.2020.0167

Pla, S., Benvenuto, C., Capellini, I., & Piferrer, F. (2022) — The most comprehensive phylogenetic analysis of sexual system evolution in fishes to date (4,614 species, 50+ orders). Shows that gonochorism is ancestral and that protogyny is the most evolutionarily stable form of hermaphroditism. Essential background for framing why protogynous species are so disproportionately represented on reefs. Nature Communications, 13, 3029. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-022-30419-z

Marshall, N. J., Cortesi, F., de Busserolles, F., Siebeck, U. E., & Cheney, K. L. (2019) — Review of color and color vision in reef fishes covering how the clear-water reef environment shapes both signal production (color patterns) and signal detection (photoreceptors). Places the male display colors of wrasses and parrotfishes in the context of their visual ecology and what receivers actually perceive. Journal of Fish Biology, 95(1), 5–38. DOI: 10.1111/jfb.13849
