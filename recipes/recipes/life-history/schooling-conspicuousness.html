Recipe 14: Schooling and Conspicuousness

Do schooling species have different color patterns from solitary species?

The Big Question

Do schooling reef fish species have more uniform, less individually distinctive color patterns than solitary species — or do they actually show higher conspicuousness, consistent with school membership relaxing the need for individual crypsis?

This Is an Instance Of…

Categorical PGLS — testing whether a discrete behavioral ecology state predicts a continuous color metric

You are asking whether the ecological strategy of group living predicts color pattern metrics after accounting for shared ancestry. This is a one-way phylogenetic ANOVA (PGLS with a binary categorical predictor). The question is genuinely two-directional: theory generates competing predictions, and finding either outcome is interpretable and interesting. The recipe below tests m_dS (achromatic contrast, relevant to the brightness-based confusion effect) and Jc (chromatic complexity) against a binary schooling variable pulled from FishBase’s ecology table.

Why This Matters for Your Clade

Schooling behavior creates two opposing selective pressures on individual color patterns:

The confusion effect (favoring conspicuousness): A dense, moving school presents predators with many identical targets and overwhelms their ability to track one individual. Individual conspicuousness — even high contrast — may be tolerated or even favored if it helps the school cohere and coordinate, making the confusion effect stronger.

The oddity effect (favoring uniformity): A phenotypically odd individual stands out from its schoolmates and becomes a disproportionate target for predators seeking a trackable individual. This selects for extreme homogeneity within schools — and could mean schooling species evolve lower pattern complexity or lower individual distinctiveness than solitary species.

Which effect dominates varies by group, predator type, and the scale of the pattern relative to viewing distance. Testing this across your clade tells you something about which of these ecological forces has had a stronger hand in shaping color evolution.

Clade-specific tips
  • Acanthuridae (surgeonfishes): This family includes species that school intensely (many Acanthurus, Ctenochaetus) alongside distinctly solitary or pair-territorial species (Zebrasoma, some Naso). Within-family contrast is strong, making this an ideal group for Recipe 14.
  • Balistoidea (triggerfishes + filefishes): Most triggerfishes are solitary and territorial, while a few filefish species can be found in loose aggregations. Sample sizes in each category may be small — check before committing.
  • Scarinae (parrotfishes): Some species feed in small loose groups, others are more solitary or harem-based. FishBase Schooling codes can be sparse for this group; check coverage before running the recipe.
  • Pomacanthidae (angelfishes): Largely solitary or pair-living; “schooling” records are rare. This recipe may not be well-powered for angelfishes specifically.
  • Cirrhilabrini / Halichoerinae / Hypsigenyinae: Moderate to good FishBase coverage. Worth trying especially for genera that span both habits.

The Method

You will pull the ecology table from FishBase for your species, extract the Schooling column, and collapse it into a binary predictor: schooling versus solitary/pairs. Species coded as “aggregating,” “loose groups,” or similar intermediate categories may need manual review — print the raw values and decide how to bin them before running the model.

The primary PGLS tests m_dS ~ schooling_binary. Achromatic contrast (m_dS) is the metric most directly relevant to the confusion-effect prediction because the dazzle effect of schooling depends on high-contrast body markings that are visible to predators across viewing distances, and predator luminance channels (green-shifted in many teleosts) are most sensitive to this axis. You can also run Jc ~ schooling_binary as a secondary test of chromatic conspicuousness.

How to Interpret Results

Result Interpretation What to say on your poster
Schooling m_dS > solitary m_dS, p < 0.05 Schooling species have more achromatic contrast “Schooling species show higher achromatic contrast, consistent with high-contrast stripe patterns facilitating school cohesion or dazzle-based predator confusion.”
Schooling m_dS < solitary m_dS, p < 0.05 Schooling species have less achromatic contrast “Schooling species show lower contrast patterns, potentially reflecting the oddity effect: uniform-looking individuals are less likely to be singled out by predators.”
No significant difference, p > 0.05 Schooling behavior does not predict achromatic contrast “We find no significant relationship between schooling behavior and achromatic contrast in our clade, suggesting color pattern may be shaped more strongly by diet or habitat than by social behavior.”
Schooling Jc > solitary Jc, p < 0.05 Schooling species are more chromatically complex “Chromatic complexity is elevated in schooling species; this may reflect species recognition signals that help fish identify and join conspecific schools.”
Common pitfalls
  • FishBase “Schooling” is a coarse field. It distinguishes schooling, aggregating, and solitary/pairs, but the boundary between “schooling” and “aggregating” is not always consistently applied across families. Print table(merged$Schooling, useNA = "always") before cleaning and look for unexpected entries.
  • “Schooling” in FishBase often refers to larvae or juveniles. Many reef fish school as larvae or small juveniles even if adults are solitary. If you can, try to verify whether the FishBase entry refers to adult behavior.
  • Low FishBase coverage for some clades. If more than 30% of your species return NA for Schooling, the cleaned binary sample will be small and potentially biased toward well-studied species. Report coverage explicitly.
  • The confusion and oddity effects may operate at different spatial scales. High-contrast horizontal stripes (like surgeonfish) could simultaneously create school-level dazzle (high m_dS within school) AND individual oddity risk if one fish in the school lacks stripes. Your PGLS tests the between-species level, not the within-school effect — keep this distinction clear in your interpretation.

The Code

# ── Recipe 14: Schooling and Conspicuousness ─────────────────────
source("../../code/helpers.R")   # loads ape, phytools, nlme, ggplot2, theme_poster

# ── 1. Load your pavo metrics and tree ──────────────────────────
traits <- read.csv("path/to/your-pavo-metrics.csv",
                   stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
tree   <- ape::read.tree("path/to/your-family-mini-tree.tre")

# ── 2. Pull schooling behavior from FishBase ─────────────────────
fb <- pull_fishbase(traits$species,
                    fields = list(ecology = c("Schooling")))

cat("Raw Schooling values from FishBase:\n")
print(table(fb$Schooling, useNA = "always"))

# ── 3. Merge and clean to binary predictor ──────────────────────
merged <- merge(traits, fb, by = "species")

# Collapse to "schooling" vs "solitary_pairs"
# Review the raw table above and adjust regex if needed for your clade
merged$schooling_binary <- ifelse(
  grepl("school", merged$Schooling, ignore.case = TRUE),
  "schooling",
  ifelse(
    grepl("solitar|pair|alone|non-school", merged$Schooling,
          ignore.case = TRUE),
    "solitary_pairs",
    NA
  )
)

cat("\nSpecies retained by schooling category:\n")
print(table(merged$schooling_binary, useNA = "always"))

# Drop species with NA or ambiguous schooling status
n_before <- nrow(merged)
merged   <- merged[!is.na(merged$schooling_binary), ]
cat("\nDropped", n_before - nrow(merged),
    "species with NA or ambiguous schooling\n")
cat("Final N:", nrow(merged), "\n")

# ── 4. Match tree to filtered data ──────────────────────────────
matched <- match_tree_data(tree, merged, species_col = "species")
cat("Species in PGLS:", nrow(matched$data), "\n")
cat("  Schooling:", sum(matched$data$schooling_binary == "schooling"), "\n")
cat("  Solitary/pairs:",
    sum(matched$data$schooling_binary == "solitary_pairs"), "\n")

# ── 5a. PGLS: achromatic contrast ~ schooling ───────────────────
model_dS <- safe_pgls(m_dS ~ schooling_binary,
                      df = matched$data, tree = matched$tree)
cat("\n── PGLS: m_dS ~ schooling_binary ──\n")
print(summary(model_dS))

# ── 5b. PGLS: chromatic complexity ~ schooling ──────────────────
model_Jc <- safe_pgls(Jc ~ schooling_binary,
                      df = matched$data, tree = matched$tree)
cat("\n── PGLS: Jc ~ schooling_binary ──\n")
print(summary(model_Jc))

# ── 6. Boxplot for m_dS ─────────────────────────────────────────
ggplot(matched$data,
       aes(x = schooling_binary, y = m_dS, fill = schooling_binary)) +
  geom_boxplot(alpha = 0.7, outlier.shape = NA) +
  geom_jitter(width = 0.15, size = 3, alpha = 0.6, color = "grey30") +
  labs(
    title = "Achromatic contrast by schooling behavior",
    x     = "",
    y     = "Achromatic transition density (m_dS)"
  ) +
  scale_x_discrete(labels = c("schooling"     = "Schooling",
                               "solitary_pairs" = "Solitary / Pairs")) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("schooling"      = "#2a9d8f",
                                "solitary_pairs" = "#e76f51")) +
  theme_poster +
  theme(legend.position = "none")

ggsave("results/recipe14-schooling-mds-boxplot.pdf",
       width = 6, height = 5)
cat("Saved: results/recipe14-schooling-mds-boxplot.pdf\n")

# ── 7. Boxplot for Jc ───────────────────────────────────────────
ggplot(matched$data,
       aes(x = schooling_binary, y = Jc, fill = schooling_binary)) +
  geom_boxplot(alpha = 0.7, outlier.shape = NA) +
  geom_jitter(width = 0.15, size = 3, alpha = 0.6, color = "grey30") +
  labs(
    title = "Chromatic complexity by schooling behavior",
    x     = "",
    y     = "Chromatic colour volume (Jc)"
  ) +
  scale_x_discrete(labels = c("schooling"     = "Schooling",
                               "solitary_pairs" = "Solitary / Pairs")) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("schooling"      = "#2a9d8f",
                                "solitary_pairs" = "#e76f51")) +
  theme_poster +
  theme(legend.position = "none")

ggsave("results/recipe14-schooling-jc-boxplot.pdf",
       width = 6, height = 5)
cat("Saved: results/recipe14-schooling-jc-boxplot.pdf\n")
