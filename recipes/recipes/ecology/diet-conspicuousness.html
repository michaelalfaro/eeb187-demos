Recipe 8: Diet and Conspicuousness

Are herbivores more or less conspicuous than carnivores?

The Big Question

Does trophic level predict how chromatically conspicuous a species is — and if so, which direction does the relationship go?

This Is an Instance Of…

Ecological niche and signal evolution

This recipe tests whether a species’ position in the food web predicts its visual signaling strategy. Diet is a fundamental ecological axis that determines foraging behavior, social structure, and exposure to predators. Herbivores, planktivores, and carnivores face different selection pressures on coloration: an ambush predator may need crypsis, while a territorial herbivore may benefit from conspicuous species-recognition signals. Testing this with PGLS asks whether these pressures leave a detectable evolutionary signature across your clade.

Why This Matters for Your Clade

Trophic level (Troph in FishBase) is a continuous variable ranging from about 2.0 (strict herbivores/detritivores) to 4.5+ (top predators). Unlike categorical diet labels, Troph is estimated from quantitative stomach content or stable isotope data, making it suitable for regression. The prediction is not straightforward: you might expect high-trophic carnivores to be less conspicuous (ambush predation favors crypsis), but many reef carnivores are brightly colored (think lionfish or groupers). Conversely, herbivorous surgeonfishes and parrotfishes are often boldly colored, possibly because their algal diet provides carotenoid pigments and their social structure favors species recognition. Your data will tell you which story holds for your clade.

Clade-specific tips
  • Acanthuridae (surgeonfishes): This family spans a wide trophic range: Naso species include planktivores (high Troph), while Zebrasoma and Acanthurus include strict algal grazers (low Troph). Both extremes include strikingly colored species — testing whether the relationship is flat would itself be interesting.
  • Balistoidea (triggerfishes + filefishes): Varied diets from sponge-eaters to hard-shelled invertebrate specialists. Triggerfishes are often boldly patterned regardless of diet, which could indicate that diet does not constrain coloration in this group.
  • Labridae (wrasses): Enormous trophic diversity, from coral-feeding Labrichthys to piscivorous Cheilinus. The broad range should give good statistical power.

The Method

We use PGLS to regress chromatic contrast (m_dS) against trophic level (Troph). No log transform is needed for Troph because it is already on a bounded, approximately linear scale (~2 to ~4.5). Chromatic contrast (m_dS) measures the average difference in saturation between adjacent color pattern elements — higher values mean more chromatically conspicuous patterns. PGLS accounts for phylogeny so that the result is not driven by, for example, all the conspicuous species being in one clade that happens to be herbivorous.

Consider also running this analysis with m_dL (achromatic/luminance contrast) as the dependent variable. Some species may be cryptic in hue but highly contrasting in brightness, or vice versa. Comparing the two results adds depth to your poster.

How to Interpret Results

Result Interpretation What to say on your poster
Negative slope, p < 0.05 Higher trophic level (more carnivorous) = less conspicuous “Carnivorous species in [family] are significantly less chromatically conspicuous than herbivores, consistent with selection for crypsis in predatory species.”
Positive slope, p < 0.05 Higher trophic level = more conspicuous “Carnivorous species in [family] are more chromatically conspicuous. This could reflect honest signaling of predator status, or a release from herbivore-specific constraints.”
p > 0.05 No significant relationship “Trophic level does not predict chromatic conspicuousness in [family] after controlling for phylogeny. Color signals may serve functions (mate choice, species recognition) that are independent of feeding ecology.”
Common pitfalls
  • Troph is an estimate, not a direct measurement. FishBase Troph values are computed from diet composition data using a standardized algorithm. Accuracy varies by species — well-studied species have better estimates. Check the number of diet studies underlying each estimate if precision concerns you.
  • Trophic level conflates diet type with diet breadth. A specialist corallivore and a generalist omnivore might have similar Troph values but very different ecologies. If your clade has complex trophic structure, consider also looking at diet composition categories from the ecology() table.
  • Consider size correction (Recipe 7b). Body size correlates with both trophic level (larger fish tend to be more carnivorous) and color. Run Recipe 7b first if you suspect size is a confound.

The Code

# ── Recipe 8: Diet and Conspicuousness ────────────────────────
source("https://michaelalfaro.github.io/eeb187-demos/recipes/code/helpers.R")

# ── 1. Load your data ────────────────────────────────────────
traits <- read.csv("path/to/your/pavo_metrics.csv",
                    stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
bundle <- load_bundle("path/to/your-family-mini-bundle")

# ── 2. Pull trophic level from FishBase ──────────────────────
fb <- pull_fishbase(traits$species,
                    fields = list(estimate = c("Troph")))

# ── 3. Merge ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
merged <- merge(traits, fb, by = "species")
merged <- merged[!is.na(merged$Troph), ]

cat("Species with trophic data:", nrow(merged), "\n")
cat("Troph range:", round(range(merged$Troph), 2), "\n")

# ── 4. Match to tree ────────────────────────────────────────
matched <- match_tree_data(bundle$tree, merged, species_col = "species")

# ── 5. PGLS: chromatic contrast vs trophic level ────────────
model <- safe_pgls(m_dS ~ Troph, df = matched$data, tree = matched$tree)

# ── 6. Scatter plot ─────────────────────────────────────────
library(ggplot2)

p <- ggplot(matched$data, aes(x = Troph, y = m_dS)) +
  geom_point(size = 4, alpha = 0.7, color = "#264653") +
  { if (!is.null(model))
    geom_abline(intercept = coef(model)[1], slope = coef(model)[2],
                color = "#e76f51", linewidth = 1.2) } +
  labs(x = "Trophic Level (Troph)",
       y = "Mean Chromatic Contrast (m_dS)",
       title = "Does diet predict conspicuousness?",
       subtitle = "PGLS controlling for phylogeny") +
  theme_poster

print(p)
ggsave("results/recipe8-diet-vs-conspicuousness.pdf", p,
       width = 10, height = 8)
cat("Saved: results/recipe8-diet-vs-conspicuousness.pdf\n")

From the Literature

Endler, J. A. (1978) — The foundational paper on predator perception and animal color patterns, demonstrating that guppy color patterns represent a compromise between sexual selection (favoring conspicuousness) and predation pressure (favoring crypsis). The framework applies broadly to any system where diet-related ecology creates differential predation regimes. Evolutionary Biology, 11, 319–364.

Marshall, N. J. (2000) — Showed that reef fish use the same “bright” colors for both communication and camouflage, depending on viewing distance and background. This challenges simple predictions about diet and conspicuousness: a species can be simultaneously conspicuous to conspecifics at short range and cryptic to predators at long range. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 355(1401), 1243–1248. DOI
