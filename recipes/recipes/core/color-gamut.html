Foundation Recipe — Building a clade color gamut from images

How much of perceptual color space does my clade actually occupy?

Start here

This is the Foundation Recipe — the first recipe every group runs. It is part of the project’s Minimum Viable Project (MVP) triad (Foundation Recipe + Recipe 1 Phylogenetic Signal + Recipe 3 Axis of Diversification). Build your clade’s color gamut here first; the other recipes assume you have it in hand.

The Big Question

If we drop every color my clade uses into a perceptually uniform color space, how big is the cloud — and how does that cloud compare to other clades?

This Is an Instance Of…

Color gamut quantification

A color gamut is the convex hull of all colors a clade realizes in some color space. Stoddard and Prum (2011) put avian plumage colors into the tetrahedral space defined by bird cone-photoreceptor responses and showed that, across birds, the family-level tetrahedral volumes vary by more than two orders of magnitude — some lineages fill a tiny corner of avian color space, others sprawl across it. The gamut volume is the single most common summary statistic for “how colorful is this clade”.

We cannot replicate the avian-tetrahedron setup for our reef-fish images (no reflectance spectra, no quantum catches, no patch-level standardized photography). What we can do is build the same conceptual object — a 3-D convex hull — in CIELAB color space from the colors that pavo::classify() extracts via k-means clustering on each species’ image. CIELAB is perceptually uniform: equal distances in the space correspond to equal perceived color differences. This makes it the standard choice for quantifying color diversity from photographic data when spectral reflectance is unavailable (Endler & Mielke, 2005).

The three CIELAB axes map directly onto the opponent-channel model from lecture:

Axis Range Meaning Biological connection
L* 0 (black) – 100 (white) Lightness Luminance channel — the achromatic signal
a* negative (green) – positive (red) Red–green opponency Corresponds to the L-minus-M cone opponency channel
b* negative (blue) – positive (yellow) Yellow–blue opponency Corresponds to the S-minus-(L+M) cone opponency channel

Two derived quantities are also useful:

  • Chroma = \(\sqrt{a^{*2} + b^{*2}}\) — how vivid/saturated the color is. Zero chroma = pure gray. High chroma = intensely colored.
  • Hue angle = \(\mathrm{atan2}(b^*, a^*)\) — the “what color” dimension. 0 degrees = red, 90 degrees = yellow, 180 degrees = green, 270 degrees = blue.

The a*–b* plane is a natural chromaticity diagram: hue is the angle from the origin and chroma (saturation) is the distance from the origin. Collapsing L* onto this plane separates lightness from chromatic content — crucial for clades where the black-bar-vs-white-body axis dominates the raw RGB cloud.

Why This Matters for Your Clade

Every poster eventually asks the reader: “is my clade colorful?” That word does a lot of work. Color richness (how many distinct colors a species shows), color contrast (how far apart neighboring colors are), and color space coverage (how much of the possible palette a clade collectively uses) are all “colorful” in plain English but they measure different things. The gamut volume is the answer to the third question: across all your species, how much of the available perceptual color space does the clade actually occupy?

Clade-specific tips
  • Acanthuridae (surgeonfishes): A genus-by-genus or subfamily-by-subfamily comparison of gamut volume is the natural follow-up — Acanthurus species span a wide gamut; Ctenochaetus species are mostly clustered in the brown/olive corner; Zebrasoma and Paracanthurus extend into bright yellows and blues that pull the hull outward.
  • Pomacanthidae (angelfishes): Juvenile and adult color phases can almost double a species’ contribution to the gamut. Decide up front whether each species is represented by its adult, its juvenile, or both — and report that choice. Mixing is fine; pretending you didn’t is not.
  • Cirrhilabrini (fairy wrasses): Strong sexual dichromatism means a males-only gamut will look very different from a females-only or mixed gamut. The male display gamut is the more interesting one for most signaling-evolution questions.
  • Balistoidea (triggerfishes + filefishes): Two-family comparison is built in — compute the gamut for Balistidae and Monacanthidae separately and put them on the same axes.

Inputs

This recipe expects:

  • One image directory with one representative image per species, the filename (minus extension) being the species’ tip label, exactly as it appears in your tree.
  • A pruned tree for your clade (Newick or NEXUS), with tip labels matching your image filenames.
  • A background-color CSV (background-ids.csv) listing, for each image (or * for “all images”), the approximate RGB centroid of the background you saw in your photos. You build this by eyeballing the centroid swatches the recipe prints in section 3 below. The smoketest ships a pre-filled one. Why RGB and not a cluster index: pavo::classify() does not return stable cluster IDs across runs (a re-knit can re-number “cluster 3” to “cluster 1”), so we annotate the color we saw, not the index it was given.
One image per species — pick the representative

Use exactly one image per species. If you have a males-and-females set, an adult-and-juvenile set, or three angles of the same fish, pick the single most representative image (usually: adult, dorsal-lateral, fully spread) and use just that one. The recipe will hard-stop with a clear error if it sees duplicate species labels in your image directory.

Multi-image aggregation (averaging centroids, weighting by image count, modeling within-species variance) is a real question — it is being broken out into a separate follow-up recipe so the gamut workflow stays uncluttered.

The Method

For each species’ image, pavo::classify() runs k-means on the pixel RGB values and returns k cluster centroids plus the pixel-count proportion that each cluster occupies. One of those clusters is almost always the background (or fin-clipped negative space, or solid white card behind the fish). The standard pavo workflow is to flag that cluster and drop it from any downstream analysis.

How we flag the background: the recipe prints a swatch grid of every cluster centroid for every image, with the centroid’s RGB triple labeled. You (the human) look at the swatches, decide what the background color of your photographs is (e.g., “blue water around RGB(0.20, 0.45, 0.65)” or “white card around RGB(0.95, 0.95, 0.95)”), and record those RGB values in background-ids.csv. For each image, the recipe then finds the cluster whose centroid is closest in Euclidean RGB distance to the bg color you recorded, and drops that cluster. This is the canonical workflow for the recipe book — it is more pedagogically transparent than any automatic heuristic, and the failure modes (“fish fills the frame”, “background is a mottled reef”, “white card is brighter than the brightest fin”) become impossible to silently miss because you’ve inspected the swatches yourself. It is also robust to k-means cluster-ID instability: a re-knit may renumber the clusters, but the bg color you recorded still matches the same centroid by nearest-neighbour.

Once backgrounds are stripped, we are left with a per-species set of foreground cluster centroids in RGB space. We then convert each RGB triple to CIELAB via the colorspace R package (sRGB -> XYZ -> L*a*b*, assuming D65 illuminant). We pool these CIELAB centroids across the whole clade and compute the 3-D convex hull with geometry::convhulln(..., options = "FA"), which returns both the hull facets and the hull volume. The volume is the headline number — the clade gamut size.

To make the volume interpretable as a percentage, we compute the volume of the entire sRGB gamut mapped into CIELAB (a reference boundary) and report the clade’s volume as a fraction of that reference. A clade with an occupancy of 10% fills 10% of the achievable camera-color space in perceptual units.

We then plot a 2-panel hero figure: (A) the a*–b* chromaticity diagram showing the clade’s chromatic diversity with a convex hull outline and concentric chroma circles, and (B) an L* vs chroma scatter showing the joint distribution of lightness and saturation.

How to Interpret Results

What you see What it means What to say on your poster
Points clustered near the center of the a*-b* plot Desaturated, grayish colors (low chroma) “The clade’s colors are predominantly unsaturated, near neutral gray.”
Points far from the center Vivid, saturated colors (high chroma) “The clade includes strongly saturated, vivid hues.”
Points in one quadrant only (e.g., +a*/+b*) Clade dominated by that color category (warm reds/yellows in this case) “The clade is chromatically specialized in the red-yellow sector.”
Points spread across multiple quadrants High chromatic diversity “The clade realizes a wide range of hue categories.”
Wide L* range in Panel B Large lightness contrast (e.g., black bars + white body) “The clade spans a broad lightness range, from dark melanin patterns to bright pale body colors.”
Gamut occupancy < 5% Tightly clustered in perceptual color space “The clade occupies a narrow region of perceptual color space — its species converge on a shared palette.”
Gamut occupancy 5% -- 25% Moderate coverage typical of many reef-fish families “Color diversity is moderate; the clade explores a portion of the achievable color space.”
Gamut occupancy > 25% Broad coverage; species range from drab to saturated and span hues “The clade fills a large region of perceptual color space, with species at multiple extremes of hue and saturation.”
Gamut volume changes a lot when you change k Sensitive to classification granularity Report the gamut for at least two values of k (e.g., k = 4 and k = 6) and discuss which more honestly summarizes your species’ patterns.
Common pitfalls and honest caveats
  • This is camera-derived color, not fish-perceived color. Reef fish do not see the way a camera does — they have UV-sensitive cones, different spectral tuning, and view their world through water that filters wavelengths. CIELAB is perceptually uniform for human viewers (which is the poster’s audience), but it does not capture what the fish themselves perceive. The gamut you compute here is “how much of human-perceptual color space the clade fills”, not “how much of fish color space”.
  • Backgrounds matter a lot. If your images have inconsistent backgrounds (blue water vs. white card vs. mottled reef), the “background cluster” may not be a clean single class and your foreground centroids will be contaminated. Crop tightly when you can, and inspect every image’s swatch grid before you write its bg cluster into background-ids.csv.
  • k is a knob. Choosing k = 3 gives you very coarse summary colors; k = 8 gives you fine ones. Larger k almost always inflates the gamut volume because more centroids span more of the space. Pick a k that matches the actual chromatic structure of your species (most reef fish: 3 to 6 patches) and report the choice.
  • One image per species is a sample of one. The recipe enforces one image per species (see Inputs above). A second image of the same species — different lighting, different angle, different life stage — will give you slightly different centroids. The gamut is a property of your image set, not a population estimate. Two groups using two different image sets for the same clade will get different volumes. Multi-image aggregation will be addressed in a follow-up recipe.
  • Cluster centroids hide within-patch variation. A patch that is a smooth gradient from yellow to orange gets summarized by a single yellow-orange centroid. If a clade’s “color story” is about gradients, the centroid-and-hull approach undersells it.

The Code

Setup

suppressPackageStartupMessages({
  library(pavo)
  library(geometry)
  library(colorspace)
  library(ape)
  library(ggplot2)
  library(patchwork)
  library(grDevices)
})

Point to YOUR bundle

Important

Change these paths to match your bundle location. Your FishView bundle has images/ with one PNG per species and a tree file ending in -tree.tre or -mini-tree.tre. If you’re using the Workbench, your bundle is in the data/ folder of your project.

# ── YOUR BUNDLE ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
# Change this ONE path to point to your FishView bundle directory:
bundle_dir <- "path/to/your/bundle"

# Everything else is derived automatically:
img_dir   <- file.path(bundle_dir, "images")
tree_file <- list.files(bundle_dir, pattern = "-tree\\.tre$",
                        full.names = TRUE)[1]

# Background-color CSV: if you haven't built one yet, the recipe will
# create a template after the swatch-inspection step below.
bg_csv <- file.path(bundle_dir, "background-ids.csv")

# k for the k-means classification. Reef-fish species typically have
# 3-6 distinct color patches plus a background, so k = 4 is a sensible
# default. Try k = 3 and k = 5 to see how sensitive your gamut is.
k <- 4L

# Alternatively, if your bundle has a species_k_bg.csv with per-species
# gestalt_k values from the FishView dashboard, load those:
k_csv <- file.path(bundle_dir, "species_k_bg.csv")
if (file.exists(k_csv)) {
  cat("Found per-species k values in species_k_bg.csv — will use those.\n")
} else {
  cat("Using fixed k =", k, "for all species.\n")
}
# -- 1a. Enforce one-image-per-species policy -----------------------------
# Every image filename (sans extension) must map to a unique species.
# If you have multiple images of a species, pick the most representative
# and use just that one. Multi-image aggregation is a follow-up recipe.
#
# IMPORTANT — HYBRID bundle layout: a FishView-exported bundle now contains
# BOTH flat exemplars (images/Genus_species.png) and per-species replicate
# folders (images/Genus_species/exemplar.png, img-1.png, ...). For the
# one-image-per-species color-gamut analysis we use ONLY the flat files:
# the pattern below matches "Genus_species.png" directly under `img_dir`
# (not recursive), which both layouts satisfy and which never picks up
# `exemplar.png` or `img-N.png` from per-species subfolders.

img_files <- list.files(
  img_dir,
  pattern = "^[A-Z][a-z]+_[a-z]+\\.(png|jpg|jpeg|tif|tiff)$",
  ignore.case = FALSE, full.names = FALSE, recursive = FALSE
)
if (length(img_files) == 0) {
  # Fall back to the broader pattern for old flat-only smoketest data.
  img_files <- list.files(img_dir,
                          pattern = "\\.(png|jpg|jpeg|tif|tiff)$",
                          ignore.case = TRUE, full.names = FALSE,
                          recursive = FALSE)
}
species_labels <- tools::file_path_sans_ext(img_files)
dup_species    <- species_labels[duplicated(species_labels)]

if (length(dup_species)) {
  stop(
    "Duplicate species labels detected in image directory:\n  ",
    paste(unique(dup_species), collapse = ", "),
    "\n\nThe color-gamut recipe requires ONE representative image per species.\n",
    "Pick the most representative image for each duplicated species and ",
    "remove the others from `", img_dir, "`. Multi-image aggregation will ",
    "be addressed in a follow-up recipe."
  )
}

cat("OK: ", length(img_files), " images, all unique species labels.\n", sep = "")
# -- 1b. Load all species images and classify each one with k-means ------
# We set a seed before classify() so the swatch grid you inspect (section 3)
# and the centroid table (section 5) are byte-identical run-to-run. k-means
# cluster indices may still shuffle if you change `k` or your image
# set; the bg-by-RGB-distance match in section 4 is robust to that.
#
# Load each flat file one-by-one so we never recurse into per-species
# replicate folders. (pavo::getimg() accepts a single path or a directory,
# but not a vector of paths -- so we lapply over the flat list and combine.)
set.seed(20260526)
img_full <- file.path(img_dir, img_files)
imgs <- lapply(img_full, function(p) pavo::getimg(p))
class(imgs) <- c("rimg", "list")
cat("Loaded", length(imgs), "images\n")

cl <- pavo::classify(imgs, kcols = k)
# -- 2. Pull per-image cluster centroids (RGB) and proportions -----------
# summary(cl) gives the RGB centroid of every cluster in every image,
# but does NOT include the proportion (pixel share). We compute the
# proportions from the classified pixel matrices directly.
centroids <- summary(cl)
names(centroids)[1] <- "img_ID"

prop_rows <- lapply(seq_along(cl), function(i) {
  m   <- cl[[i]]
  tab <- prop.table(table(as.vector(m)))
  data.frame(
    img_ID     = attr(m, "imgname"),
    col_ID     = as.integer(names(tab)),
    proportion = as.numeric(tab),
    stringsAsFactors = FALSE
  )
})
props <- do.call(rbind, prop_rows)

per_image <- merge(centroids, props, by = c("img_ID", "col_ID"))
per_image$species <- per_image$img_ID  # filename was set to tip label
head(per_image)
# -- 3. Print a swatch grid of every centroid for every image ------------
# This is the picture you read while filling in `background-ids.csv`.
# Each row is one image; each cell shows the cluster's centroid colour
# with its col_ID printed on top in contrasting text. The student's
# job: for each row, decide which col_ID is the background and write
# that pair into the CSV.

swatch_df <- per_image
swatch_df$hex <- grDevices::rgb(swatch_df$R, swatch_df$G, swatch_df$B,
                                maxColorValue = 1)
# pick a readable text colour per swatch (white on dark, black on light)
swatch_df$text_col <- ifelse(
  (0.299 * swatch_df$R + 0.587 * swatch_df$G + 0.114 * swatch_df$B) > 0.55,
  "black", "white"
)
swatch_df$label <- sprintf("(%.2f, %.2f, %.2f)\n%.0f%%",
                          swatch_df$R, swatch_df$G, swatch_df$B,
                          100 * swatch_df$proportion)

ggplot(swatch_df, aes(x = factor(col_ID), y = img_ID, fill = hex)) +
  geom_tile(colour = "grey20", linewidth = 0.3) +
  geom_text(aes(label = label, colour = text_col), size = 2.6, lineheight = 0.9) +
  scale_fill_identity() +
  scale_colour_identity() +
  labs(x = "Cluster (this run's index)", y = NULL,
       title = "Centroid swatches per image",
       subtitle = "Identify the background COLOR (RGB triple) and record it in background-ids.csv") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 11) +
  theme(panel.grid = element_blank(),
        axis.text.y = element_text(family = "mono"))
# -- 4. Drop the background cluster for each image, via nearest-RGB ------
# Convention (recipe-book canonical): you (the student) inspect the
# swatch grid above and record, for each image, the approximate RGB
# triple of the background colour you saw. For each image we then
# drop the cluster whose centroid is closest in Euclidean RGB distance
# to your recorded bg colour. This is robust to k-means cluster-index
# instability across knits.
#
# Schema for background-ids.csv (lines starting with `#` are ignored):
#   img_ID,bg_R,bg_G,bg_B
#   Acanthurus_achilles,0.55,0.55,0.55
#   ...
# Use `img_ID = "*"` as a wildcard row that applies to every image
# without an explicit entry (handy when all your photos share a
# common background, e.g., a uniform white card).

if (!file.exists(bg_csv)) {
  stop(
    "Background-color CSV not found at:\n  ", bg_csv,
    "\n\nCreate it by inspecting the swatch grid printed above and recording,",
    " for each image (or `*` for all), the bg colour you saw. Schema:\n",
    "  img_ID,bg_R,bg_G,bg_B\n  Acanthurus_achilles,0.55,0.55,0.55\n  ...\n"
  )
}

bg_map <- read.csv(bg_csv, comment.char = "#", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
required_cols <- c("img_ID", "bg_R", "bg_G", "bg_B")
missing_cols  <- setdiff(required_cols, names(bg_map))
if (length(missing_cols)) {
  stop("background-ids.csv is missing required column(s): ",
       paste(missing_cols, collapse = ", "),
       ".\nExpected schema: img_ID,bg_R,bg_G,bg_B")
}

# Per-image bg lookup: explicit row beats wildcard row.
wildcard <- bg_map[bg_map$img_ID == "*", ]
specific <- bg_map[bg_map$img_ID != "*", ]

# Sanity check: every image must be covered by either a specific row
# or a wildcard row.
covered_imgs <- unique(c(specific$img_ID,
                         if (nrow(wildcard)) unique(per_image$img_ID) else character(0)))
uncovered    <- setdiff(unique(per_image$img_ID), covered_imgs)
extra_in_csv <- setdiff(specific$img_ID, unique(per_image$img_ID))
if (length(uncovered)) {
  stop("background-ids.csv does not cover these images (no specific row ",
       "and no wildcard `*` row):\n  ",
       paste(uncovered, collapse = ", "))
}
if (length(extra_in_csv)) {
  warning("background-ids.csv lists images not in the image directory:\n  ",
          paste(extra_in_csv, collapse = ", "))
}

# For each image, find the centroid closest to the recorded bg RGB.
per_image$is_background <- FALSE
match_log <- vector("list", length(unique(per_image$img_ID)))
for (idx in seq_along(unique(per_image$img_ID))) {
  im <- unique(per_image$img_ID)[idx]
  # Pick the bg target: specific row preferred, else wildcard.
  hit <- specific[specific$img_ID == im, ]
  if (nrow(hit) == 0) hit <- wildcard
  bg_rgb <- c(hit$bg_R[1], hit$bg_G[1], hit$bg_B[1])

  rows <- per_image[per_image$img_ID == im, ]
  d <- sqrt((rows$R - bg_rgb[1])^2 +
            (rows$G - bg_rgb[2])^2 +
            (rows$B - bg_rgb[3])^2)
  bg_col_id <- rows$col_ID[which.min(d)]
  per_image$is_background[per_image$img_ID == im &
                          per_image$col_ID == bg_col_id] <- TRUE
  match_log[[idx]] <- data.frame(
    img_ID    = im,
    bg_R      = round(rows$R[which.min(d)], 3),
    bg_G      = round(rows$G[which.min(d)], 3),
    bg_B      = round(rows$B[which.min(d)], 3),
    distance  = round(min(d), 3),
    stringsAsFactors = FALSE
  )
}
matches <- do.call(rbind, match_log)

cat("Matched background centroids (nearest RGB to recorded bg colour):\n")
print(matches, row.names = FALSE)

foreground <- per_image[!per_image$is_background, ]
cat("\nMarked ", sum(per_image$is_background), " background centroids; ",
    "kept ", nrow(foreground), " foreground centroids across ",
    length(unique(foreground$species)), " species.\n", sep = "")
# -- 5. Tidy per-species centroid table (what you'd put in supp.) --------
species_table <- foreground[, c("species", "R", "G", "B", "proportion")]
species_table <- species_table[order(species_table$species,
                                     -species_table$proportion), ]
knitr::kable(species_table, digits = 3,
             caption = "Foreground RGB centroids per species (background dropped).")
# -- 6. Convert RGB to CIELAB --------------------------------------------
# The colorspace package converts sRGB (gamma-corrected camera RGB) to
# CIELAB via the XYZ intermediate, assuming D65 illuminant. This is the
# standard conversion for photographs stored as sRGB JPEG/PNG.
pts_rgb <- as.matrix(foreground[, c("R", "G", "B")])
srgb_obj <- colorspace::sRGB(pts_rgb[, "R"], pts_rgb[, "G"], pts_rgb[, "B"])
lab_obj  <- as(srgb_obj, "LAB")
lab_pts  <- colorspace::coords(lab_obj)
colnames(lab_pts) <- c("L", "a", "b")

# Attach CIELAB coordinates to the foreground data
foreground$L <- lab_pts[, "L"]
foreground$a <- lab_pts[, "a"]
foreground$b <- lab_pts[, "b"]
foreground$chroma <- sqrt(foreground$a^2 + foreground$b^2)
foreground$hue_deg <- (atan2(foreground$b, foreground$a) * 180 / pi) %% 360

cat(sprintf("L* range: [%.1f, %.1f]\n", min(lab_pts[, "L"]), max(lab_pts[, "L"])))
cat(sprintf("a* range: [%.1f, %.1f]\n", min(lab_pts[, "a"]), max(lab_pts[, "a"])))
cat(sprintf("b* range: [%.1f, %.1f]\n", min(lab_pts[, "b"]), max(lab_pts[, "b"])))
cat(sprintf("Chroma range: [%.1f, %.1f]\n",
            min(foreground$chroma), max(foreground$chroma)))
# -- 7. Convex hull in CIELAB space + volume -----------------------------
hull_lab <- geometry::convhulln(lab_pts, options = "FA")
vol_lab  <- hull_lab$vol

# Reference volume: the entire sRGB gamut boundary mapped into CIELAB.
# We sample the sRGB cube on a fine grid and compute its convex hull.
srgb_grid <- expand.grid(R = seq(0, 1, by = 0.05),
                          G = seq(0, 1, by = 0.05),
                          B = seq(0, 1, by = 0.05))
ref_srgb  <- colorspace::sRGB(srgb_grid$R, srgb_grid$G, srgb_grid$B)
ref_lab   <- colorspace::coords(as(ref_srgb, "LAB"))
hull_ref  <- geometry::convhulln(ref_lab, options = "FA")
vol_ref   <- hull_ref$vol

occupancy_lab <- vol_lab / vol_ref

cat(sprintf("Clade CIELAB gamut volume:       %.0f cubic CIELAB units\n", vol_lab))
cat(sprintf("sRGB reference volume in CIELAB: %.0f cubic CIELAB units\n", vol_ref))
cat(sprintf("Occupancy (vs sRGB gamut):       %.1f%%\n", 100 * occupancy_lab))
# -- 8. Hero figure: a*-b* chromaticity + L* vs chroma ------------------
# Panel A: a*-b* chromaticity diagram with convex hull + chroma circles
# Panel B: L* vs chroma scatter showing lightness distribution

plot_df <- data.frame(
  species = foreground$species,
  L       = foreground$L,
  a       = foreground$a,
  b       = foreground$b,
  chroma  = foreground$chroma,
  hue_deg = foreground$hue_deg,
