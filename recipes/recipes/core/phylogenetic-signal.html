Recipe 1: Phylogenetic Signal

Is color pattern complexity conserved across the phylogeny of my family, or does it evolve rapidly?

The Big Question

Do closely related species in my family share similar color patterns, or has color evolved so fast that the phylogeny tells us nothing about who looks like whom?

This Is an Instance Of…

Phylogenetic signal testing

This is the most fundamental question in phylogenetic comparative biology: does the evolutionary tree predict trait similarity? Phylogenetic signal quantifies whether close relatives resemble each other more than expected by chance. It is the starting point for nearly every comparative analysis because it determines how you should interpret all downstream results — if signal is strong, you must account for shared ancestry in every statistical test; if signal is weak, the trait is evolving fast enough that history alone does not constrain it.

Why This Matters for Your Clade

Color patterns serve many functions on a reef — camouflage, species recognition, mate attraction, warning signals. Whether these functions produce conserved or rapidly evolving patterns depends on the ecological and social pressures acting on each lineage. Testing for phylogenetic signal tells you whether your family’s color diversity is the product of deep evolutionary inertia or recent, lineage-specific innovation.

Clade-specific tips
  • Pomacanthidae (angelfishes): Dramatic ontogenetic color changes (juveniles look completely different from adults) might weaken signal if your images mix life stages. Make sure all exemplars are adults.
  • Cirrhilabrini (fairy wrasses): Strong sexual dichromatism means male display colors may have different signal than female cryptic colors. If your bundle has mostly males, your K values describe male display evolution.
  • Balistoidea (triggerfishes + filefishes): This group spans two families with very different body plans. Expect signal to differ depending on whether the triggerfishes or filefishes dominate your species sample.

The Method

We use two complementary statistics. Blomberg’s K compares the observed variance of a trait on the tree to the variance expected if the trait evolved by Brownian motion (random drift along branches). K = 1 means the trait matches Brownian motion exactly. K > 1 means close relatives are more similar than drift predicts — the trait is conserved. K < 1 means close relatives are less similar than drift predicts — the trait is evolving faster than expected, perhaps driven by natural or sexual selection, or by convergent evolution across distantly related lineages. A permutation test (shuffling trait values across tips 1,000 times) produces a p-value testing whether K is significantly different from zero (no signal at all).

Pagel’s lambda asks the same question from a different angle. It transforms the tree’s internal branch lengths until the tree best fits the observed trait data. Lambda = 1 means the original tree is optimal — full phylogenetic signal. Lambda = 0 means a “star phylogeny” (all species equally unrelated) fits just as well — no signal. Values between 0 and 1 indicate partial signal. A likelihood-ratio test produces the p-value.

Using both metrics is standard practice because they are sensitive to different aspects of the data. K is more influenced by tip-level similarity (it detects whether sister species resemble each other), while lambda is more sensitive to deep phylogenetic structure (it detects whether major clades differ). If both are significant, you can confidently claim phylogenetic signal. If they disagree, that disagreement is itself informative about where on the tree the signal lives.

How to Interpret Results

Result Interpretation What to say on your poster
K < 1, p < 0.05 Significant signal, but weaker than Brownian motion “Color evolves faster than expected under drift — selection or convergence is reshaping patterns across the tree.”
K ~ 1, p < 0.05 Signal consistent with Brownian motion “Color diversity is consistent with neutral evolutionary drift along the phylogeny.”
K > 1, p < 0.05 Signal stronger than Brownian motion “Color is phylogenetically conserved — close relatives look strikingly similar, more so than drift alone would produce.”
p > 0.05 No significant signal detected “We lack statistical evidence for phylogenetic signal, either because it is genuinely absent or because our sample is too small to detect it.”
Common pitfalls
  • “Non-significant does not mean no signal.” With fewer than ~20 species, K tests have low statistical power. A non-significant result might simply mean you cannot distinguish signal from noise with your sample size — not that signal is absent. Always report the K value alongside the p-value, and note your sample size.
  • “K < 1 does NOT mean color doesn’t matter.” K < 1 means color is less phylogenetically structured than Brownian motion predicts. That is often the most interesting result — it implies selection, adaptation, or convergence is actively driving color evolution beyond what shared ancestry would produce.
  • Do not compare raw K values across variables without context. K for transition density (m) and K for aspect ratio (A) are on different scales because the traits themselves have different distributions. Compare the pattern (significant vs. not) rather than the raw numbers.

The Code

# ── Recipe 1: Phylogenetic Signal ───────────────────────────────
source("https://michaelalfaro.github.io/eeb187-demos/recipes/code/helpers.R")   # loads ape, phytools, ggplot2, theme_poster

# ── 1. Load your bundle and pavo data ───────────────────────────
bundle   <- load_bundle("path/to/your-family-mini-bundle")
pavo_csv <- read.csv("path/to/your-pavo-metrics.csv",
                      stringsAsFactors = FALSE)

# ── 2. Match tree to data ───────────────────────────────────────
matched <- match_tree_data(bundle$tree, pavo_csv, species_col = "species")
tree    <- matched$tree
dat     <- matched$data

cat("Working with", length(tree$tip.label), "species\n")

# ── 3. Blomberg's K for all 6 pavo variables ───────────────────
K_table <- format_K_table(dat, tree,
                          vars = c("m", "A", "Jc", "Jt", "m_dS", "m_dL"))
print(K_table)

# ── 4. Pagel's lambda for comparison ───────────────────────────
lambda_results <- data.frame(
  Variable = character(0),
  lambda   = numeric(0),
  p_value  = numeric(0),
  stringsAsFactors = FALSE
)

for (v in c("m", "A", "Jc", "Jt", "m_dS", "m_dL")) {
  x <- setNames(dat[[v]], dat$species)
  x <- x[!is.na(x)]
  # Prune tree to species with data for this variable
  overlap  <- intersect(names(x), tree$tip.label)
  t_pruned <- ape::drop.tip(tree, setdiff(tree$tip.label, overlap))
  x <- x[t_pruned$tip.label]

  res <- phytools::phylosig(t_pruned, x, method = "lambda", test = TRUE)
  lambda_results <- rbind(lambda_results, data.frame(
    Variable = v,
    lambda   = round(res$lambda, 3),
    p_value  = round(res$P, 4),
    stringsAsFactors = FALSE
  ))
}
print(lambda_results)

# ── 5. Run a quick PCA to get PC1 for the contMap ──────────────
pavo_vars <- c("m", "A", "Jc", "Jt", "m_dS", "m_dL")
trait_mat <- dat[, pavo_vars]
rownames(trait_mat) <- dat$species

# Remove any rows with NA before PCA
complete <- complete.cases(trait_mat)
trait_mat <- trait_mat[complete, ]
tree_pca  <- ape::drop.tip(tree, setdiff(tree$tip.label,
                                          rownames(trait_mat)))
trait_mat <- trait_mat[tree_pca$tip.label, ]

pc <- prcomp(trait_mat, scale. = TRUE)
pc1 <- setNames(pc$x[, "PC1"], rownames(pc$x))

# ── 6. contMap: paint PC1 onto the tree (fan layout) ───────────
cm <- phytools::contMap(tree_pca, pc1, plot = FALSE)

# On-screen plot
plot(cm, type = "fan", fsize = 0.5, lwd = 3,
     legend = 0.7 * max(nodeHeights(tree_pca)))
title("PC1 of color pattern mapped on phylogeny")

# ── 7. Save a poster-quality PDF ───────────────────────────────
pdf("results/recipe1-contmap-pc1.pdf", width = 10, height = 10)
plot(cm, type = "fan", fsize = 0.5, lwd = 3,
     legend = 0.7 * max(nodeHeights(tree_pca)))
title("PC1 of color pattern mapped on phylogeny")
dev.off()
cat("Saved: results/recipe1-contmap-pc1.pdf\n")

# ── 8. Print a combined summary table ──────────────────────────
summary_table <- merge(K_table, lambda_results, by = "Variable",
                       suffixes = c("_K", "_lambda"))
print(summary_table)

From the Literature

Blomberg, S. P., Garland, T., Jr., & Ives, A. R. (2003) — Introduced the K statistic and permutation test for phylogenetic signal, showing that behavioral traits tend to be more evolutionarily labile than morphological ones. Evolution, 57(4), 717–745. DOI

Pagel, M. (1999) — Developed the lambda transformation for estimating the degree to which shared evolutionary history explains trait similarity among species. Nature, 401(6756), 877–884. DOI

Munkemuller, T., Lavergne, S., Bzeznik, B., Dray, S., Jombart, T., Schiffers, K., & Thuiller, W. (2012) — Compared the performance of K, lambda, and other phylogenetic signal indices across simulated trees, providing practical guidance on when each metric is most reliable. Methods in Ecology and Evolution, 3(4), 743–756. DOI
