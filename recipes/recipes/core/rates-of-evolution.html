Recipe 2: Rates of Evolution

Do color patterns in my family evolve faster than expected under Brownian motion?

The Big Question

How fast is color pattern evolving in my family compared to other reef fish families — and what does that speed tell us about the forces shaping color diversity?

This Is an Instance Of…

Evolutionary rate inference

Comparing observed rates of trait evolution to theoretical expectations and to published benchmarks is a core task in macroevolution. Brownian motion provides the null model: traits drifting at a constant rate along the tree. When K is much less than 1, the trait is evolving faster than drift alone would produce, pointing to directional selection, adaptive radiation, or convergent evolution. By placing your family’s K values next to published values from other reef fish families, you can ask whether your clade’s color evolution is typical or exceptional.

Why This Matters for Your Clade

Reef fish families differ enormously in how quickly their color patterns evolve. Butterflyfishes (Chaetodontidae) show very weak phylogenetic signal — color changes rapidly, with distantly related species converging on similar patterns. If your family shows similarly low K values, the same ecological forces (mimicry, habitat matching, rapid speciation) may be at work. If your family shows much higher K, something is constraining color evolution — perhaps strong stabilizing selection or developmental limits on pattern variation.

Clade-specific tips
  • Acanthuridae (surgeonfishes): Your family was analyzed by Frederich et al. using binary pattern traits. Compare your pavo-based K values to their results for a methods comparison (binary motif coding vs. continuous adjacency statistics).
  • Scarinae (parrotfishes): Parrotfish undergo dramatic color changes between initial and terminal phase. If your bundle uses terminal-phase males, your K values describe the rate of evolution of the most sexually selected color state.
  • Halichoerine (Coris-allies): With ~140 tips, this is the largest group. Larger trees generally give tighter K estimates, so your comparisons to published benchmarks are on firmer statistical ground.

The Method

This recipe builds directly on Recipe 1. You already have K values for each pavo variable. The new step is to place those values in context by comparing them to published benchmarks — K values from the same pavo variables measured on other reef fish families.

The key benchmark comes from Alfaro et al. (2019), who measured the same six pavo adjacency statistics on Chaetodontidae (butterflyfishes) and reported per-variable K values from a phyloPCA. Their PC1 K was 0.122, PC2 K was 0.192, and PC3 K was 0.136 — all far below 1, indicating that butterflyfish color patterns evolve much faster than Brownian motion predicts. For Acanthuridae, Frederich et al. reported a multivariate K of approximately 0.10 using binary pattern coding, which is consistent with the butterflyfish story of fast color evolution.

The comparison is qualitative, not a formal statistical test. You are asking: does my family’s K sit in the same low-signal zone as butterflyfishes, or does it diverge? A bar plot with your K values next to the published benchmarks makes this comparison visual and immediate. If your K values cluster near the benchmarks, your family follows the same “fast color evolution” narrative. If they are notably higher, your family may be under different evolutionary pressures that conserve color patterns.

How to Interpret Results

Your K vs. benchmarks Interpretation What to say on your poster
Your K similar to Chaetodontidae (~0.1–0.2) Your family’s color evolves at a similar fast rate “Color pattern evolution in [family] follows the fast-evolution pattern seen in butterflyfishes, consistent with rapid adaptation or convergence.”
Your K much lower than benchmarks (< 0.1) Even faster evolution than butterflyfishes “Color in [family] evolves exceptionally fast — even less phylogenetically structured than the already-labile butterflyfish patterns.”
Your K much higher than benchmarks (> 0.5) Slower evolution, more phylogenetically conserved “Unlike butterflyfishes, [family] retains ancestral color patterns — close relatives look similar, suggesting stabilizing selection or developmental constraints.”
K varies widely across pavo variables Different aspects of color evolve at different rates “Color evolution in [family] is mosaic: some pattern features (e.g., aspect ratio) are conserved while others (e.g., complexity) evolve rapidly.”
Common pitfalls
  • Your K and the published K are not on identical scales unless measured the same way. Alfaro et al. (2019) used the same pavo adjacency statistics and phyloPCA, so the comparison is appropriate for PC-level K. Frederich et al. used binary motif presence/absence and a multivariate K, which is a different estimand. Note this distinction on your poster.
  • Sample size affects K. A family with 15 species will produce noisier K estimates than one with 60 species. The comparison is most informative if your species count is in the same ballpark as the benchmark studies.
  • Do not over-interpret small differences. K = 0.15 vs. K = 0.12 is not a meaningful difference. Focus on order-of-magnitude contrasts (K ~ 0.1 vs. K ~ 0.8).

The Code

# ── Recipe 2: Rates of Evolution ────────────────────────────────
source("https://michaelalfaro.github.io/eeb187-demos/recipes/code/helpers.R")   # loads ape, phytools, ggplot2, theme_poster

# ── 1. Load your bundle and pavo data ───────────────────────────
bundle   <- load_bundle("path/to/your-family-mini-bundle")
pavo_csv <- read.csv("path/to/your-pavo-metrics.csv",
                      stringsAsFactors = FALSE)

# ── 2. Match tree to data ───────────────────────────────────────
matched <- match_tree_data(bundle$tree, pavo_csv, species_col = "species")
tree    <- matched$tree
dat     <- matched$data

# ── 3. Compute K for all 6 pavo variables ──────────────────────
K_table <- format_K_table(dat, tree,
                          vars = c("m", "A", "Jc", "Jt", "m_dS", "m_dL"))

# ── 4. Build the benchmark data frame ──────────────────────────
# Published K values from Alfaro et al. 2019 (Chaetodontidae, pavo)
# and Frederich et al. (Acanthuridae, binary motifs — multivariate K)
benchmarks <- data.frame(
  family   = c("Chaetodontidae", "Chaetodontidae", "Chaetodontidae",
               "Acanthuridae"),
  variable = c("PC1", "PC2", "PC3", "multivariate"),
  K        = c(0.122, 0.192, 0.136, 0.10),
  source   = c(rep("Alfaro et al. 2019", 3), "Frederich et al."),
  stringsAsFactors = FALSE
)

# ── 5. Combine your results with benchmarks ─────────────────────
your_family <- gsub("-mini-bundle", "",
                    basename(normalizePath("path/to/your-family-mini-bundle")))
my_K <- data.frame(
  family   = your_family,
  variable = K_table$Variable,
  K        = K_table$K,
  source   = "Your analysis",
  stringsAsFactors = FALSE
)

compare_df <- rbind(benchmarks, my_K)
compare_df$label <- paste(compare_df$family, compare_df$variable, sep = " — ")

# ── 6. Bar plot: your K vs. published benchmarks ───────────────
p <- ggplot(compare_df,
            aes(x = reorder(label, K), y = K, fill = source)) +
  geom_col(width = 0.7) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 1, linetype = "dotted", color = "grey40") +
  coord_flip(ylim = c(0, max(compare_df$K, 1) * 1.15)) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("Alfaro et al. 2019" = "#d4a853",
                                "Frederich et al."   = "#a0c4e8",
                                "Your analysis"      = "#0a66c2")) +
  labs(
    title    = paste("Blomberg's K:", your_family, "vs. published benchmarks"),
    subtitle = "Dotted line = K = 1 (Brownian motion expectation)",
    x = NULL, y = "Blomberg's K", fill = "Source"
  ) +
  theme_poster

print(p)

# ── 7. Save poster-quality PDF ──────────────────────────────────
ggsave("results/recipe2-K-comparison.pdf", p, width = 12, height = 8)
cat("Saved: results/recipe2-K-comparison.pdf\n")

# ── 8. Print interpretation text ────────────────────────────────
cat("\n── Interpretation ──\n")
avg_K <- mean(K_table$K, na.rm = TRUE)
if (avg_K < 0.2) {
  cat("Your family's average K (", round(avg_K, 3), ") is in the same\n",
      "low-signal zone as Chaetodontidae. Color evolves fast.\n")
} else if (avg_K < 0.5) {
  cat("Your family's average K (", round(avg_K, 3), ") shows moderate\n",
      "signal — more conserved than butterflyfishes but still below BM.\n")
} else {
  cat("Your family's average K (", round(avg_K, 3), ") is notably higher\n",
      "than Chaetodontidae. Color patterns are phylogenetically conserved.\n")
}

From the Literature

Alfaro, M. E., Karan, E. A., Schwartz, S. T., & Shultz, A. J. (2019) — Quantified color pattern geometry in butterflyfishes using pavo adjacency statistics and found weak phylogenetic signal (K ~ 0.12–0.19), indicating rapid, convergent color evolution. Integrative and Comparative Biology, 59(3), 604–615. DOI

Blomberg, S. P., Garland, T., Jr., & Ives, A. R. (2003) — Established the K statistic framework and showed that morphological traits typically have higher K than behavioral traits across vertebrates, providing the theoretical baseline for rate comparisons. Evolution, 57(4), 717–745. DOI

Harmon, L. J., Losos, J. B., Davies, T. J., Gillespie, R. G., Gittleman, J. L., Jennings, W. B., … & Mooers, A. O. (2010) — Found that early bursts of morphological evolution are rare across animal clades, suggesting that most trait diversification accumulates gradually rather than in ancient adaptive radiations. Evolution, 64(8), 2385–2396. DOI
