Recipe 19: Disparity Through Time

Did color diversity accumulate early or late in my family’s history?

The Big Question

Did the major axes of color pattern variation in my family originate early in its history and then slow down, or have color patterns continued to diversify at a steady pace all the way to the present?

This Is an Instance Of…

Macroevolutionary tempo analysis

Disparity-through-time (DTT) analysis asks how morphological (or color) diversity was distributed across the tree at each moment in evolutionary history. It works by computing, for each internal node, the average pairwise disparity within subclades descending from that node, then watching how that subclade disparity changes as you move from the root toward the tips. If subclades partition color space early — each major lineage staking out its own region — disparity within subclades will be low relative to total disparity, and you get a negative MDI. If subclades overlap in color space and diversification is ongoing, disparity within subclades stays high relative to total disparity, and you get a positive MDI. This is one of the few macroevolutionary methods that directly tests whether an adaptive radiation “front-loaded” its diversity.

Why This Matters for Your Clade

Reef fish families differ enormously in whether their color diversity looks early-burst or gradual. Some families, like angelfishes, have iconic deep-clade differences in color pattern (adults vs. juveniles, planktivores vs. cleaners) that were established millions of years ago. Others, like wrasses, show more recent explosions of color in sexual signals. DTT analysis gives you a visual and quantitative answer to which pattern dominates in your family. It also connects directly to lectures on macroevolutionary rates (Lec 18) — you are putting your family’s color evolution on the same framework used to study entire adaptive radiations.

Clade-specific tips
  • Pomacanthidae (angelfishes): The juvenile–adult color difference is a developmental, not evolutionary, pattern — make sure your pavo metrics all come from adult photographs. If early diverging genera (e.g., Pomacanthus) have very different color from derived genera (e.g., Centropyge), expect a negative MDI: early lineages staked out very different color space.
  • Cirrhilabrini (fairy wrasses): The extreme color diversity in this tribe is largely concentrated in Cirrhilabrus and Paracheilinus. Whether this shows as early or late on the DTT curve depends on where the tribe-level split falls relative to the species-level diversification within these genera.
  • Hypsigenyinae (hogfishes and allies): With only ~38 species, your DTT null envelope will be wide. A non-significant MDI does not mean the pattern is absent — report MDI and interpret it qualitatively even if the p-value is borderline.
  • Balistoidea (triggerfishes + filefishes): The two families (Balistidae and Monacanthidae) diverged early and have quite different color strategies. If your bundle spans both, expect early-burst signal from that deep split.

The Method

DTT uses your pavo trait matrix and your time-calibrated phylogeny to compute subclade disparity at each internal node. The dtt() function in the geiger package does the heavy lifting. It then compares the observed DTT curve against a Brownian motion null, generated by simulating 1,000 random datasets on the same tree. The morphological disparity index (MDI) is the signed area between the observed DTT curve and the Brownian motion median. Positive MDI means your observed disparity stays higher than expected under drift (subclades overlap — late-filling color space); negative MDI means your observed disparity falls below expected (subclades are partitioned — early-burst pattern).

The input should be a matrix where rows are species and columns are your pavo variables (m, A, Jc, Jt, m_dS, m_dL). Using all six variables jointly captures overall color pattern disparity rather than any single axis.

This analysis requires the geiger package. If it is not installed, run install.packages("geiger") once before sourcing helpers.

How to Interpret Results

MDI Meaning What to say on your poster
MDI > 0 (p < 0.05) Subclades overlap in color space — ongoing diversification, clades have not partitioned color axes “Color pattern diversity accumulated gradually and continuously; major clades have not converged on distinct color strategies.”
MDI < 0 (p < 0.05) Subclades partitioned — early niche partitioning, each major clade occupies distinct color space “Color pattern diversity shows an early-burst signature: major color axes were established early in the family’s history and subsequent diversification filled within-clade space.”
MDI ~ 0 (p > 0.05) Observed trajectory matches Brownian motion expectation “Color pattern disparity accumulates at a rate consistent with neutral evolutionary drift, without strong evidence for either early burst or recent acceleration.”
p-value marginally significant (0.05–0.10) Borderline Report MDI and direction; note that your sample size may limit power.
Common pitfalls
  • DTT requires a time-calibrated (ultrametric) tree. Check that your tree is ultrametric before running: ape::is.ultrametric(tree). If it returns FALSE, small numerical errors from tree-building may be to blame; try phytools::force.ultrametric(tree) to correct them, and report that you did so.
  • Interpret the MDI in light of your tree shape. A heavily pectinate (ladder-shaped) tree will produce different null expectations than a balanced tree. The Brownian motion simulation envelope accounts for this, but if your tree is very asymmetric, the envelope will be wide and power will be lower.
  • Missing data affects disparity estimates. If some species lack values for one or more pavo variables, remove them before building the trait matrix. Do not impute missing values for this analysis.
  • DTT is a whole-tree summary. It does not tell you which node drove the early burst. If you want to identify the responsible clade, compare DTT curves for subclades separately (run dtt() on pruned subtrees).

The Code

# ── Recipe 19: Disparity Through Time ───────────────────────────
# Requires the geiger package.
# If not installed: install.packages("geiger")

source("../../code/helpers.R")   # loads ape, phytools, ggplot2, theme_poster
library(geiger)

# ── 1. Load your tree and pavo data ─────────────────────────────
bundle   <- load_bundle("path/to/your-family-mini-bundle")
pavo_csv <- read.csv("path/to/your-pavo-metrics.csv",
                      stringsAsFactors = FALSE)

# ── 2. Match tree to data ───────────────────────────────────────
matched <- match_tree_data(bundle$tree, pavo_csv, species_col = "species")
tree    <- matched$tree
dat     <- matched$data

cat("Working with", length(tree$tip.label), "species\n")

# ── 3. Check that tree is ultrametric ───────────────────────────
if (!ape::is.ultrametric(tree)) {
  cat("Tree is not ultrametric — forcing ultrametric (small numerical fix)\n")
  tree <- phytools::force.ultrametric(tree, method = "extend")
}

# ── 4. Build trait matrix ───────────────────────────────────────
pavo_vars <- c("m", "A", "Jc", "Jt", "m_dS", "m_dL")
complete  <- complete.cases(dat[, pavo_vars])
if (any(!complete)) {
  cat("Dropping", sum(!complete), "species with missing pavo values\n")
}
dat_complete <- dat[complete, ]
tree         <- ape::drop.tip(tree,
                              setdiff(tree$tip.label, dat_complete$species))

trait_mat            <- as.matrix(dat_complete[, pavo_vars])
rownames(trait_mat)  <- dat_complete$species
trait_mat            <- trait_mat[tree$tip.label, ]   # align row order

cat("Final sample for DTT:", nrow(trait_mat), "species\n")

# ── 5. DTT analysis with 1000 simulations ───────────────────────
set.seed(42)   # for reproducibility
dtt_result <- dtt(tree, trait_mat, nsim = 1000, plot = FALSE)

# ── 6. Compute p-value and report MDI ─────────────────────────────
# geiger's dtt() returns MDI but not always a p-value (depends on
# package version). We compute it from the simulation matrix:
# for each BM simulation, compute its MDI, then ask what fraction
# of simulated MDIs are >= the observed MDI.
bm_median <- apply(dtt_result$sim, 1, median, na.rm = TRUE)
sim_mdis  <- apply(dtt_result$sim, 2, function(s) mean(s - bm_median))
obs_mdi   <- as.numeric(dtt_result$MDI)
mdi_pval  <- mean(sim_mdis >= obs_mdi)

cat("\n── Disparity-Through-Time results ──\n")
cat("MDI (morphological disparity index):", round(obs_mdi, 3), "\n")
cat("p-value (proportion of BM sims with MDI >= observed):",
    round(mdi_pval, 3), "\n")

if (obs_mdi > 0) {
  cat("Interpretation: subclades OVERLAP in color space (MDI > 0)\n")
  cat("  Color diversity has accumulated continuously; no early partitioning.\n")
} else {
  cat("Interpretation: subclades are PARTITIONED in color space (MDI < 0)\n")
  cat("  Early-burst signature: major color axes established early.\n")
}

# ── 7. Helper function to plot a DTT result ───────────────────────
# geiger 2.0.11 does not provide a plot method for dtt objects,
# so we draw the DTT envelope manually.
plot_dtt <- function(dtt_obj, main = "Disparity Through Time", ...) {
  times     <- dtt_obj$times
  obs_curve <- dtt_obj$dtt
  sim_lower <- apply(dtt_obj$sim, 1, quantile, probs = 0.025, na.rm = TRUE)
  sim_upper <- apply(dtt_obj$sim, 1, quantile, probs = 0.975, na.rm = TRUE)
  sim_med   <- apply(dtt_obj$sim, 1, median, na.rm = TRUE)

  plot(times, obs_curve, type = "n",
       ylim = range(c(obs_curve, sim_lower, sim_upper), na.rm = TRUE),
       main = main,
       xlab = "Relative time (root = 0, tips = 1)",
       ylab = "Average subclade disparity", ...)
  polygon(c(times, rev(times)), c(sim_lower, rev(sim_upper)),
          col = rgb(0, 0, 0, 0.1), border = NA)
  lines(times, sim_med, col = "grey50", lty = 2)
  lines(times, obs_curve, col = "black", lwd = 2)
}

# ── 7b. Save DTT plot as PDF ─────────────────────────────────────
pdf("results/recipe19-dtt.pdf", width = 8, height = 6)
plot_dtt(dtt_result,
         main = "Disparity Through Time — Color Pattern")
mtext(paste0("MDI = ", round(obs_mdi, 3),
             "  (p = ", round(mdi_pval, 3), ")"),
      side = 1, line = -2, adj = 0.05, cex = 0.9)
dev.off()
cat("Saved: results/recipe19-dtt.pdf\n")

# ── 8. Optional: DTT for individual pavo variables ──────────────
# Run DTT separately for each variable to see which drives the pattern.
pdf("results/recipe19-dtt-by-variable.pdf", width = 12, height = 8)
par(mfrow = c(2, 3))
for (v in pavo_vars) {
  x_vec            <- setNames(dat_complete[[v]], dat_complete$species)
  x_vec            <- x_vec[tree$tip.label]
  x_mat            <- matrix(x_vec, ncol = 1,
                             dimnames = list(names(x_vec), v))
  dtt_v <- tryCatch(
    dtt(tree, x_mat, nsim = 200, plot = FALSE),
    error = function(e) NULL
  )
  if (!is.null(dtt_v)) {
    bm_med_v   <- apply(dtt_v$sim, 1, median, na.rm = TRUE)
    sim_mdis_v <- apply(dtt_v$sim, 2, function(s) mean(s - bm_med_v))
    mdi_v      <- as.numeric(dtt_v$MDI)
    pval_v     <- mean(sim_mdis_v >= mdi_v)

    plot_dtt(dtt_v, main = v)
    mtext(paste0("MDI=", round(mdi_v, 2), " p=", round(pval_v, 2)),
          side = 1, line = -1.5, adj = 0.05, cex = 0.75)
  }
}
dev.off()
cat("Saved: results/recipe19-dtt-by-variable.pdf\n")

From the Literature

Harmon, L. J., Schulte, J. A., II, Larson, A., & Losos, J. B. (2003) — Introduced the disparity-through-time framework and the MDI statistic by applying it to iguanian lizard radiations. Showed that Anolis lizards show a classic adaptive radiation pattern (early partitioning of ecomorphs) while other iguanians do not. This paper is the primary reference for the DTT method. Science, 301(5635), 961–964. DOI

Slater, G. J., Price, S. A., Santini, F., & Alfaro, M. E. (2010) — Applied DTT analysis to body size evolution in elopomorph fishes, showing that this diverse lineage does not show an early-burst pattern despite being an ancient radiation. A good example of how to report and interpret a non-significant or positive MDI result. Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 277(1687), 1563–1569. DOI

Melville, J., Harmon, L. J., & Losos, J. B. (2006) — Used DTT to compare color and morphological disparity in Ctenotus skinks, demonstrating that color and body shape can show different macroevolutionary tempos within the same clade — a useful precedent for interpreting divergent results across your six pavo variables. Evolution, 60(8), 1577–1587. DOI
