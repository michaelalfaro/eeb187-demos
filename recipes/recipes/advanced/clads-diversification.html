Recipe 21: ClaDS1 — Lineage-Specific Diversification Rates

Do diversification rates vary across lineages in my family, and which lineages are diversifying fastest?

The Big Question

Do diversification rates vary across lineages in my family, and which lineages are diversifying fastest?

This Is an Instance Of…

Diversification rate heterogeneity

Most models of species diversification assume a single rate for the entire tree, but real clades rarely diversify at a constant pace. Some lineages explode with new species while others stagnate. ClaDS (Cladogenetic Diversification rate Shift) models allow the speciation rate to change at every single node in the tree, producing a branch-by-branch portrait of where diversification has been fast and where it has been slow. This is one of the most detailed views of diversification rate variation available in comparative biology.

Why This Matters for Your Clade

Every reef fish family has lineages that seem to have generated species rapidly (think of the species-rich Cirrhilabrus within wrasses, or the Centropyge dwarfs within angelfishes) and lineages that have barely diversified at all. ClaDS1 lets you quantify this variation rather than relying on intuition from looking at tree shape. The branch-specific rates it produces can also feed into downstream analyses — for example, testing whether faster-diversifying lineages have different color patterns (see Recipe 26).

Clade-specific tips
  • Pomacanthidae (angelfishes): Expect the Centropyge radiation to show elevated tip rates relative to the larger-bodied genera. If alpha < 1, the whole family may show decelerating diversification consistent with early ecological opportunity.
  • Cirrhilabrini (fairy wrasses): The tribe’s species-rich genera (Cirrhilabrus, Paracheilinus) should stand out with high tip rates. Compare these to the rest of the Labridae if your tree includes outgroup context.
  • Balistoidea (triggerfishes + filefishes): The Monacanthidae (filefishes) have far more species than Balistidae. ClaDS will quantify whether this reflects genuinely higher speciation rates or just a longer time for accumulation.
  • Acanthuridae (surgeonfishes): A relatively species-poor family — ClaDS may reveal uniformly low rates, or a single burst in one subclade (e.g., Acanthurus).
  • Hypsigenyinae (hogfishes and allies): With ~38 species, your tree is small for ClaDS. Results will have wider uncertainty — report them honestly and note the sample size limitation.

The Method

ClaDS1 is a Bayesian model implemented in the RPANDA package. Unlike models that assume a few large rate shifts (like BAMM), ClaDS allows the speciation rate to change at every speciation event. Each daughter lineage inherits a modified rate from its parent, and the model estimates how rates shift across the entire tree.

ClaDS1 estimates four key quantities. The trend parameter (alpha) tells you whether rates systematically increase or decrease through time: alpha < 1 means rates decelerate (early burst of diversification), alpha = 1 means no trend, and alpha > 1 means acceleration. The stochasticity parameter (sigma) describes how much rates jump at each node. A global extinction rate (mu) applies across the whole tree (ClaDS1 assumes constant extinction, unlike ClaDS2 which allows variable extinction). Finally, branch-specific speciation rates (lambda_i) give you a rate for every single branch in the phylogeny.

The model is fit using MCMC sampling, which means it takes significant computation time (20–60 minutes for a typical reef fish family tree of 60–100 tips). You must check that the MCMC chains have converged before interpreting results.

Runtime warning

ClaDS1 takes 20–60 minutes for a tree with 60–100 tips. Start this analysis early in your workflow and work on other recipes while it runs. Save the results object so you do not need to re-run it.

How to Interpret Results

Parameter Value Meaning
alpha < 1 Rates decelerate Early burst of diversification — speciation was faster in the past
alpha ~ 1 No trend Speciation rates have been roughly constant through time
alpha > 1 Rates accelerate Late burst — speciation is speeding up toward the present
sigma near 0 Low stochasticity Rates change little between parent and daughter — relatively homogeneous
sigma large High stochasticity Rates jump substantially at each node — heterogeneous diversification
mu near 0 Low extinction Few lineages have gone extinct relative to the number that originated
Common pitfalls
  • MCMC convergence: Always check the trace plots with plot_ClaDS_chains(). If the chains have not converged (traces still trending, not mixing around a stable value), increase the number of iterations. Do not interpret results from unconverged chains.
  • Small trees (<30 tips): ClaDS may lack statistical power to reliably estimate branch-specific rates. Report your results cautiously and note the sample size limitation.
  • RPANDA installation: If RPANDA fails to install, try: install.packages('RPANDA', repos = 'https://cran.r-project.org'). On some systems you may also need to install system dependencies for C++ compilation.
  • Runtime: ClaDS1 takes 20–60 minutes for ~60–100 tips. Start it running and work on other recipes while you wait. Save the output with saveRDS() so you never have to re-run it.

The Code

# ── Recipe 21: ClaDS1 — Lineage-Specific Diversification Rates ──
# Requires the RPANDA package.
# If not installed: install.packages("RPANDA")

source("https://michaelalfaro.github.io/eeb187-demos/recipes/code/helpers.R")   # loads ape, phytools, ggplot2, theme_poster
library(RPANDA)

# ── 1. Load your tree and data ────────────────────────────────────
bundle   <- load_bundle("path/to/your-family-mini-bundle")
pavo_csv <- read.csv("path/to/your-pavo-metrics.csv",
                      stringsAsFactors = FALSE)

# ── 2. Match tree to data ─────────────────────────────────────────
matched <- match_tree_data(bundle$tree, pavo_csv, species_col = "species")
tree    <- matched$tree
dat     <- matched$data

cat("Working with", length(tree$tip.label), "species\n")

# ── 3. Check that tree is ultrametric ─────────────────────────────
if (!ape::is.ultrametric(tree)) {
  cat("Forcing ultrametric tree (small numerical correction)\n")
  tree <- phytools::force.ultrametric(tree, method = "extend")
}

# ── 4. Run ClaDS1 ────────────────────────────────────────────────
# ClaDS1: variable speciation, constant extinction
# This takes 20-60 minutes depending on tree size — be patient!
cat("Starting ClaDS1 MCMC — this will take 20-60 minutes...\n")
cat("Start time:", format(Sys.time()), "\n")

set.seed(42)   # for reproducibility (fit_ClaDS has no seed argument)

clads1_res <- fit_ClaDS(tree,
                         sample_fraction = 1,     # 1.0 = complete sampling
                         iterations = 5e6,        # 5 million MCMC steps
                         thin = 10000,
                         file_name = "my_clads1", # backup file (NULL to disable)
                         model_id = "ClaDS1",
                         nCPU = 1)

cat("Finished at:", format(Sys.time()), "\n")

# ── 5. Save the result so you never have to re-run ───────────────
saveRDS(clads1_res, "results/clads1-result.rds")
cat("Saved: results/clads1-result.rds\n")

# ── 6. Check MCMC convergence ─────────────────────────────────────
# ALWAYS inspect trace plots before interpreting results
pdf("results/recipe21-mcmc-traces.pdf", width = 10, height = 8)
plot_ClaDS_chains(clads1_res)
dev.off()
cat("Saved: results/recipe21-mcmc-traces.pdf\n")
cat("CHECK: Do the traces look stationary (flat, mixing well)?\n")
cat("If they are still trending, increase iterations and re-run.\n")

# ── 7. Extract MAP (maximum a posteriori) estimates ───────────────
# burn = 1/2 discards first half of MCMC as burn-in
maps <- getMAPS_ClaDS(clads1_res, burn = 1/2, thin = 1)

# maps contains: [1] sigma, [2] alpha, [3] epsilon, [4] lambda_0,
# then branch-specific rates in edge-matrix order
sigma_map    <- maps[1]   # rate stochasticity
alpha_map    <- maps[2]   # rate trend (alpha < 1 = deceleration)
mu_map       <- maps[3]   # turnover rate (epsilon)
lambda0_map  <- maps[4]   # initial speciation rate
branch_rates <- maps[-(1:4)]   # per-branch speciation rates

cat("\n── ClaDS1 Results ──────────────────────────────────────────\n")
cat("Trend (alpha):", round(alpha_map, 3),
    ifelse(alpha_map < 1, "(rates decelerating)",
    ifelse(alpha_map > 1, "(rates accelerating)", "(no trend)")), "\n")
cat("Stochasticity (sigma):", round(sigma_map, 4), "\n")
cat("Extinction (mu):", round(mu_map, 4), "\n")
cat("Rate range:", round(range(branch_rates), 4), "\n")

# ── 8. Plot rate tree — branches colored by speciation rate ───────
pdf("results/recipe21-rate-tree.pdf", width = 10, height = 10)
plot_ClaDS_phylo(tree, branch_rates, lwd = 2.5, log = TRUE)
title("ClaDS1: branch-specific speciation rates")
dev.off()
cat("Saved: results/recipe21-rate-tree.pdf\n")

# ── 9. Extract tip-specific rates ────────────────────────────────
# Branch rates are in tree$edge order. Tip branches are those where
# the child node (edge[,2]) is a tip (numbered 1..N).
n_tips     <- length(tree$tip.label)
tip_edges  <- which(tree$edge[, 2] <= n_tips)
tip_rates  <- branch_rates[tip_edges]
names(tip_rates) <- tree$tip.label[tree$edge[tip_edges, 2]]
check_trait(tip_rates, tree = tree)

# Sort by rate for inspection
tip_rate_df <- data.frame(
  species = names(tip_rates),
  rate    = tip_rates,
  genus   = sub("_.*", "", names(tip_rates)),
  stringsAsFactors = FALSE
)
tip_rate_df <- tip_rate_df[order(tip_rate_df$rate, decreasing = TRUE), ]
tip_rate_df$species <- factor(tip_rate_df$species,
                               levels = tip_rate_df$species)

cat("\n── Top 10 fastest-diversifying tips ────────────────────────\n")
print(head(tip_rate_df, 10), row.names = FALSE)

cat("\n── Bottom 5 slowest-diversifying tips ──────────────────────\n")
print(tail(tip_rate_df, 5), row.names = FALSE)

# ── 10. Bar chart of tip rates colored by genus ──────────────────
ggplot(tip_rate_df, aes(x = species, y = rate, fill = genus)) +
  geom_col(alpha = 0.85) +
  coord_flip() +
  labs(title = "ClaDS1 tip-specific speciation rates",
       x = "", y = "Speciation rate (events / Myr)") +
  theme_poster +
  theme(axis.text.y = element_text(size = 7, face = "italic"),
        legend.position = "right")

ggsave("results/recipe21-tip-rates-barplot.pdf", width = 10, height = 12)
cat("Saved: results/recipe21-tip-rates-barplot.pdf\n")

# ── 11. Save tip rates for use in Recipe 26 ──────────────────────
write.csv(tip_rate_df, "results/clads1-tip-rates.csv", row.names = FALSE)
cat("Saved: results/clads1-tip-rates.csv\n")
cat("You can use these tip rates in Recipe 26 (Rate-Trait Correlation).\n")

From the Literature

Maliet, O., Hartig, F., & Morlon, H. (2019) — Introduced the ClaDS family of models, which allow speciation rates to shift at every cladogenesis event rather than at a few discrete breakpoints. Applied to birds, they showed that diversification rates are remarkably heterogeneous even within small clades, and that the “many small shifts” model outperforms “few large shifts” approaches. Nature Ecology & Evolution, 3(7), 1086–1092. DOI

Morlon, H., Lewitus, E., Condamine, F. L., Manceau, M., Clavel, J., & Drury, J. (2016) — Presented the RPANDA package for macroevolutionary analyses on phylogenetic trees, including tools for fitting diversification models, computing phylogenetic distances, and identifying branching patterns. The package provides the R implementation of ClaDS used in this recipe. Methods in Ecology and Evolution, 7(5), 589–597. DOI
