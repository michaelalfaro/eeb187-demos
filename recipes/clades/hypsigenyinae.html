Natural History

The Hypsigenyinae (hogfishes, sheephead wrasses, and tuskfishes) is a subfamily of Labridae comprising the genera Bodianus (~44 species), Choerodon (~25 species, tuskfishes), Semicossyphus (2 species, sheepheads), Clepticus (2 species, Creole wrasses), and several smaller genera. Your tree includes 38 species drawn from this clade. Molecular phylogenetics has revealed that Bodianus as traditionally defined is not monophyletic without the inclusion of Semicossyphus and Clepticus (Santini et al., 2016), and Gomon (2006) recognized 10 subgenera within Bodianus based on morphological characters. The first multi-locus time-calibrated phylogeny for the group placed the origin of the hogfish radiation in the Early Miocene, approximately 20 million years ago (Santini et al., 2016).

Hypsigenyines are hard-prey specialists. Bodianus species use their robust canine teeth to seize crabs, snails, sea urchins, and brittlestars, often carrying prey items to rock surfaces to crush them. Choerodon tuskfishes have taken this feeding specialization a step further: multiple species have been documented using rocks as anvils to break open bivalve shells — one of the best-documented examples of tool use in any fish (Jones et al., 2011; Bernardi, 2012). Choerodon schoenleinii on the Great Barrier Reef grasps a clam in its jaws, carries it to a favored anvil rock, and delivers alternating blows by rapidly rotating its body until the shell cracks. This cognitively complex behavior suggests that hypsigenyines may possess more sophisticated spatial cognition than is typical for reef fishes, an observation that connects intriguingly (if speculatively) to their often-elaborate coloration.

One of the most striking ecological features of this clade is its extraordinary depth range. While most reef fish families concentrate in the upper 30 m of the water column, hypsigenyines span from the intertidal zone (juvenile Semicossyphus pulcher, California sheephead, occurs in shallow tidepools) to well below 200 m (Bodianus species have been collected from deep rocky reefs and seamounts). This depth range is among the broadest of any labrid clade and provides a powerful natural experiment for testing how the ambient light environment shapes color evolution.

Reproductive biology follows the standard labrid pattern of protogynous hermaphroditism. A critical connection to color evolution is the cleaning behavior of juvenile Bodianus species. Many Bodianus juveniles serve as cleaner fish, adopting distinctive “cleaning livery” coloration — typically a bicolor pattern of dark upper body and pale lower body, often with a conspicuous lateral stripe — that advertises their cleaning services to larger reef fishes. This cleaning livery is lost during ontogeny as the fish transitions to the adult color pattern. The presence or absence of juvenile cleaning behavior and its associated livery is a tractable discrete trait for phylogenetic comparative analysis.

This is the smallest student group (2 students), with 38 species in the tree. While 38 species is adequate for most phylogenetic comparative methods, you should be aware that PGLS regressions and model-fitting procedures have reduced statistical power with smaller sample sizes. Focus on strong biological hypotheses and consider supplementing quantitative results with qualitative pattern descriptions.

Color Pattern Diversity

Hypsigenyine coloration is diverse and often dramatic. Bodianus rufus (Spanish hogfish) displays a bold bicolor pattern: deep purple-blue anterior grading to bright yellow posterior. B. pulchellus (spotfin hogfish) shows vivid red-orange with white banding. Semicossyphus pulcher (California sheephead) undergoes one of the most extreme color transformations in the family: juveniles are bright orange-red with a white lateral stripe, females are uniform pink, and terminal-phase males are black head and tail with a broad red midbody band.

Tropical Bodianus species tend toward vivid reds, oranges, and yellows with contrasting dark markings, while temperate species (Semicossyphus, some deep-water Bodianus) are more often uniform pink, red, or dark reddish-brown. Choerodon tuskfishes display a different aesthetic: many are green, blue-green, or olive with complex facial markings and variable banding patterns.

The juvenile cleaning livery represents a distinct “color regime” that cuts across species boundaries. Juvenile cleaners in Bodianus typically show a dark dorsum and pale ventrum with a horizontal stripe — a pattern convergent with the juvenile livery of unrelated cleaner fishes (e.g., Labroides cleaner wrasses). This convergence across distantly related lineages is strong evidence that cleaning livery is shaped by client-fish recognition rather than phylogenetic inheritance.

Dimorphism and Ontogeny Warnings

Data quality: what to watch for

IP/TP dimorphism — moderate but present. Hypsigenyines are protogynous hermaphrodites, and most species show some degree of IP/TP color difference. The dimorphism is typically less extreme than in Scarinae or Cirrhilabrini, but it is real:

  • Semicossyphus pulcher: TP males are unmistakable (black/red/black tricolor), while IPs (females) are uniform pink. Use TP males consistently.
  • Bodianus species: TP males tend to be more intensely colored with bolder markings. IP females are often similar in pattern but with muted colors. The difference is subtle enough in many species that either phase could be used, but be consistent within your dataset.
  • Choerodon: Dimorphism is variable. Some species show notable IP/TP differences (especially in facial markings), while others are nearly monomorphic.

Rule of thumb: Use TP male images when available. If you cannot determine sex from the image, use the most intensely colored individual available for each species.

Juvenile cleaning livery: Juvenile Bodianus with cleaning livery look dramatically different from adults. The bicolor pattern (dark dorsum / pale ventrum with lateral stripe) is a cleaning advertisement, not the species’ “real” color pattern for comparative purposes. Exclude cleaning-livery juveniles from your pavo analysis. They will cluster together in color space regardless of species identity, biasing your results.

How to recognize juvenile cleaners:

  • Small body size (typically < 5 cm).
  • Bold bicolor pattern: dark (black, brown, or deep blue) upper body and white or yellow lower body, often with a prominent horizontal stripe.
  • May be photographed near or on the body of a larger fish.

Small sample size: With 38 species, your statistical power for PGLS and model-comparison analyses is limited. Consider:

  • Using simpler models (BM vs. OU rather than multi-regime OU).
  • Emphasizing effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than p-values alone.
  • Supplementing quantitative analysis with qualitative pattern descriptions and phylogenetic trait mapping.

Promising Questions for This Clade

  1. Depth and Color — Hypsigenyinae has the broadest depth range of any student clade, spanning from the intertidal to beyond 200 m. This is arguably the single best group for testing the depth–color hypothesis. Do deep-dwelling species show reduced red/orange chroma and increased blue-shifted reflectance?

  2. Discrete Trait Mapping — Map the presence/absence of juvenile cleaning livery onto the phylogeny. How many times has cleaning behavior (and its associated coloration) evolved in Bodianus? Is the cleaning livery pattern phylogenetically conserved or labile?

  3. Body Size and Color Complexity — With body sizes ranging from small cleanerfish Bodianus (~10 cm) to the massive California sheephead (~90 cm), test whether larger species display different color metrics.

  4. Cross-Family Comparison — Compare hypsigenyine color diversity to the other labrid clades (Scarinae, Cirrhilabrini, Halichoerine) to test whether the crusher feeding guild constrains color evolution relative to planktivores or herbivores.
