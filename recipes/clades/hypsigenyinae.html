Hypsigenyinae — Hogfishes, Tuskfishes, and Sheepheads

Taxonomy and Phylogenetics

Hypsigenyinae is one of the early-branching subfamilies within Labridae, the wrasses and parrotfishes. Westneat and Alfaro (2005) produced the first comprehensive molecular phylogeny of Labridae using nuclear and mitochondrial markers, recovering Hypsigenyinae (including Bodianus, Choerodon, Lachnolaimus, Semicossyphus, and Clepticus) as a distinct clade near the base of the family. This basal position is biologically important: hypsigenyines retain several plesiomorphic features (large body size, robust jaws, generalist crushing morphology) that have been lost or modified in the more derived labrid subfamilies such as Cheilininae, Halichoerinae, and Scarinae.

The clade comprises roughly 90 described species. Bodianus is the most species-rich genus (~44 species), distributed in tropical and warm-temperate seas worldwide. Choerodon (~25 species, tuskfishes) is centered in the Indo-West Pacific. Lachnolaimus contains a single species, the Atlantic hogfish L. maximus. Semicossyphus (2 species) includes the California sheephead S. pulcher and the Asian sheephead S. reticulatus. Clepticus (2 species, Creole wrasses) are mid-water planktivores, and Decodon contains a handful of deep-reef species.

Phylogenetic analyses have repeatedly recovered Bodianus as paraphyletic with respect to Semicossyphus and Clepticus (Santini, Sorenson, & Alfaro, 2016). Gomon (2006) recognized ten subgenera of Bodianus based on morphology, and Santini et al. (2016) produced the first multi-locus, time-calibrated phylogeny for the hogfishes, dating the origin of the Bodianus radiation to the Early Miocene (~20 Ma). Brownstein et al. (2025) embedded hypsigenyines in a broader analysis of reef-fish diversification near the Miocene–Pliocene boundary, finding that several reef-fish clades — including labrids — underwent rate accelerations coincident with the modern expansion of coral reefs.

Natural History

Hypsigenyines are durophagous (hard-prey) predators. The clade is dominated by benthic foragers that use robust canine teeth and powerful pharyngeal jaws to subdue mollusks, crustaceans, sea urchins, and brittlestars (Westneat & Alfaro, 2005). Bodianus species typically forage by lifting rocks, biting prey, and crushing armored items in the pharyngeal mill. Choerodon tuskfishes have taken this strategy further: multiple species use rocks as anvils to break open bivalve shells, one of the best-documented examples of tool use in any fish (Jones, Brown, & Gardner, 2011; Bernardi, 2012). The behavior involves grasping a clam, swimming to a favored anvil rock, and delivering alternating blows by rapidly rotating the body until the shell cracks. Pryor and Milton (2019) reviewed the evidence for tool use across teleosts and concluded that anvil use in Choerodon meets the strictest behavioral definitions of the term, with implications for our understanding of fish cognition.

The clade is also notable for its extraordinary depth range. While most labrid clades concentrate in the upper 30 m, hypsigenyines span from the intertidal (juvenile Semicossyphus pulcher in California tidepools) to deep mesophotic reefs and seamounts beyond 200 m (some Bodianus and Decodon species). This depth range exceeds that of any other labrid subfamily and provides a powerful natural experiment for testing how the ambient light environment shapes color evolution (Stieb et al., 2023).

Reproduction follows the standard labrid pattern of protogynous hermaphroditism with an initial-phase (IP) / terminal-phase (TP) life-history transition. Females mature first, may spawn as IP fish, and then change sex to become TP males that defend territories and engage in pair spawning (Warner & Swearer, 1991). The size and social conditions that trigger sex change vary widely across the clade and remain an active area of research (Kuwamura et al., 2002). Many Bodianus species also exhibit a striking ontogenetic shift in feeding ecology: juveniles serve as cleaner fish, removing ectoparasites from larger reef fishes before transitioning to the adult crushing niche (Cote, 2000, in Harborne & Tholan, 2016).

Visual Ecology

The visual ecology of hypsigenyines is shaped by two strong selective pressures: their broad depth range and their reliance on visually mediated social signals during IP/TP transitions. Stieb et al. (2023) analyzed long-wavelength-sensitive (LWS) opsin expression across reef fishes, including labrids, and found that species occupying deeper or more turbid habitats tend to downregulate red-sensitive opsins — a pattern consistent with the spectral shift toward blue-green light at depth. Hypsigenyines, with members spanning 0–200+ m, are an ideal group for testing this prediction at fine taxonomic resolution.

Gerlach et al. (2016) characterized visual pigments and fluorescence in pseudocheilinid wrasses, providing a methodological template for studying opsin expression and reflectance in their hypsigenyine relatives. Although direct opsin characterization for Bodianus and Choerodon remains limited, the broader labrid literature suggests that wrasses generally possess multiple cone opsins (SWS1, SWS2, RH2, LWS) supporting tetra- or pentachromatic vision, with the relative expression of these opsins tunable to local light environments (Stieb et al., 2023).

The cleaning ecology of juvenile Bodianus adds a second layer of visual-ecological interest. Cleaner fish and their clients communicate visually using stereotyped color patterns — typically a bold horizontal stripe on a contrasting background — that advertise cleaning services across species boundaries (Harborne & Tholan, 2016). The convergent evolution of similar livery in unrelated cleaner lineages (e.g., juvenile Bodianus, Labroides cleaner wrasses, and gobies) is strong evidence that cleaning patterns are shaped by client-fish perception rather than phylogenetic inheritance.

Color Pattern Diversity

Hypsigenyine coloration is diverse and often spectacular. Bodianus rufus (Spanish hogfish, Caribbean) displays a bold bicolor pattern: deep purple-blue anterior grading to bright yellow posterior. B. pulchellus (spotfin hogfish) shows vivid red-orange with white banding. B. diana and related Indo-Pacific species combine red bodies with white spots and dark blotches. The Atlantic hogfish Lachnolaimus maximus is famously plastic, capable of changing color and pattern over seconds to minutes to match reef substrates — a capability tied directly to the dermal photoreception system discussed below.

Semicossyphus pulcher (California sheephead) undergoes one of the most extreme color transformations in the family: juveniles are bright orange-red with a white lateral stripe flanked by black spots; females are uniform pink with a white chin; terminal-phase males have a black head and tail with a broad red midbody band and a white chin. Choerodon tuskfishes display a different aesthetic: many species are green, blue-green, or olive with complex facial markings, prominent blue or yellow patches around the eyes, and variable trunk banding. Clepticus (Creole wrasse) is uniformly purple-blue, consistent with its mid-water planktivorous niche, where countershading and a blue-shifted background spectrum favor monochromatic blue livery (Floeter et al., 2018).

Temperate hypsigenyines (Semicossyphus, some deep-water Bodianus) tend toward muted pinks, reds, and reddish-browns. This temperate–tropical color contrast within a single clade is unusual among reef fishes and offers another tractable comparative test of how habitat shapes color.

Sexual Dimorphism, Ontogeny, and Polymorphism

Hypsigenyines are protogynous hermaphrodites, and most species exhibit IP/TP color differences (Warner & Swearer, 1991; Kuwamura et al., 2002). The magnitude of dimorphism varies widely:

  • Semicossyphus pulcher: TP males (black/red/black tricolor) are unmistakably different from IPs (uniform pink females). This is one of the most extreme IP/TP transitions in Labridae.
  • Bodianus spp.: TP males tend to be more intensely colored, with bolder markings and brighter background hues. IP females are often similar in pattern but with muted colors. Differences are subtle in some species (e.g., B. rufus) and pronounced in others (e.g., B. axillaris).
  • Choerodon spp.: Dimorphism is variable. Some species (e.g., C. fasciatus) show striking facial markings in TP males; others are nearly monomorphic.
  • Lachnolaimus maximus: Adult color is highly labile in both sexes due to chromatophore-mediated rapid color change; “dimorphism” in the conventional fixed-pattern sense is therefore difficult to characterize (Schweikert et al., 2018).

In addition to IP/TP differences, juvenile Bodianus species typically display cleaning livery that is dramatically different from the adult pattern — usually a dark dorsum and pale ventrum bisected by a conspicuous lateral stripe. This pattern is functional (it advertises cleaning services to client fish) rather than diagnostic of species identity (Harborne & Tholan, 2016; Estienne et al., 2024).

Deep Dive: Dermal Photoreception in Lachnolaimus maximus

The most exciting recent discovery in hypsigenyine visual ecology — and arguably in fish visual biology generally — comes from work on the Atlantic hogfish Lachnolaimus maximus. Hogfish are renowned for rapid color change: an individual can shift from pale white to mottled brown to bold red within seconds, matching the appearance of nearby substrate even when the fish cannot see its own body. How does the animal coordinate this color change without visual feedback from its own skin?

Schweikert et al. (2018) first characterized opsin expression in L. maximus skin using transcriptomics, finding that the short-wavelength-sensitive opsin SWS1 — normally associated with cone photoreceptors in the retina — is expressed in the dermis as well. This was the first hint that hogfish skin might be light-sensitive. Schweikert, Grace, and colleagues (2023) followed up with a direct test of this hypothesis using immunohistochemistry, electrophysiology, and behavioral assays. They demonstrated that SWS1 protein is localized to cells beneath the chromatophore layer, in a position perfectly suited to monitor the light that has passed through (and been filtered by) the overlying pigment cells. When chromatophores expand or contract, they alter the spectrum of light reaching the SWS1-expressing cells, providing a real-time readout of the fish’s own color state.

This system — which the authors describe as “skin vision” or chromatophore-coupled photoreception — represents a fundamentally new mode of sensory feedback in vertebrate color change. It is conceptually analogous to the dermal photoreception long known from cephalopods, but with a different molecular architecture and a clear evolutionary independence. Crucially, the hogfish system links visual ecology directly to color pattern evolution: rapid color change requires not only chromatophore machinery but also a sensory feedback loop, and SWS1 in the skin appears to be that loop.

Several questions remain open and are excellent targets for the comparative work this clade enables:

  1. Is dermal SWS1 expression unique to Lachnolaimus, or is it more widespread in hypsigenyines? Several Bodianus species also show rapid color change (e.g., during cleaning interactions or aggressive displays). Testing skin opsin expression across the clade would reveal whether this is a single derived feature or an ancestral hypsigenyine trait.
  2. Does the presence of dermal photoreception correlate with the magnitude of color-pattern lability across species? Species with rapid color change (Lachnolaimus, some Bodianus) would be predicted to show higher dermal opsin expression than fixed-pattern species (e.g., Semicossyphus pulcher TP males).
  3. How does dermal photoreception interact with depth? SWS1 is tuned to UV/violet wavelengths that attenuate rapidly with depth; deep-dwelling hypsigenyines might show shifts toward longer-wavelength dermal opsins (RH2, LWS).

These hypotheses are not currently testable with pavo and a phylogeny alone, but they provide rich background context for interpreting any color-evolution patterns you recover in your analysis.

Data Quality Warnings

Data quality: what to watch for

IP/TP dimorphism — moderate to strong, depending on species. Hypsigenyines are protogynous hermaphrodites, and most species show IP/TP color differences. The dimorphism is typically less extreme than in Scarinae or Cirrhilabrini but is real and can bias analyses:

  • Semicossyphus pulcher: TP males (black/red/black) and IP females (uniform pink) are essentially different “color phenotypes.” Use TP males consistently.
  • Bodianus species: TP males tend to be more intensely colored. The difference is subtle in some species but pronounced in others. Be consistent within your dataset.
  • Choerodon: Variable dimorphism. Use TP males where identifiable.

Rule of thumb: Use TP male images when available. If you cannot determine sex from the image, use the most intensely colored adult available for each species.

Juvenile cleaning livery. Juvenile Bodianus with cleaning livery look dramatically different from adults — typically a dark dorsum / pale ventrum bisected by a lateral stripe. This bicolor pattern is a cleaning advertisement (Harborne & Tholan, 2016), not the species’ “real” color pattern for comparative purposes. Exclude cleaning-livery juveniles from your pavo analysis. They will cluster together in color space regardless of species identity, biasing your results toward false convergence.

How to recognize juvenile cleaners:

  • Small body size (typically < 5 cm).
  • Bold bicolor pattern: dark (black, brown, or deep blue) upper body and white or yellow lower body, often with a prominent horizontal stripe.
  • May be photographed near or on the body of a larger client fish.

Rapid color change in Lachnolaimus. Adult hogfish can change color within seconds. A single individual photographed in different contexts can appear pale white, mottled brown, or vivid red. Choose a representative “neutral” image (mid-tone, mottled) if multiple are available, and note in your methods that Lachnolaimus coloration is plastic.

Small sample size. With 38 species, your statistical power for PGLS and model-comparison analyses is limited. Consider:

  • Using simpler models (BM vs. OU rather than multi-regime OU).
  • Emphasizing effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than p-values alone.
  • Supplementing quantitative analysis with qualitative pattern descriptions and phylogenetic trait mapping.

Taxonomic instability in Bodianus. Bodianus is paraphyletic with respect to Semicossyphus and Clepticus (Santini et al., 2016). If you use a tree that pre-dates the Santini et al. (2016) analysis, branch lengths and topology may be inaccurate. Confirm your tree source.

Promising Questions for This Clade

  1. Depth and Color — Hypsigenyinae has the broadest depth range of any student clade, spanning from the intertidal to beyond 200 m. This is arguably the single best group for testing the depth–color hypothesis (Stieb et al., 2023). Do deep-dwelling species show reduced red/orange chroma and increased blue-shifted reflectance?

  2. Discrete Trait Mapping — Map the presence/absence of juvenile cleaning livery onto the phylogeny. How many times has juvenile cleaning behavior (and its associated coloration) evolved in Bodianus? Is the cleaning livery pattern phylogenetically conserved or labile? Compare to the cleaner-mimic literature on Aspidontus and Plagiotremus (Estienne et al., 2024).

  3. Body Size and Color Complexity — With body sizes ranging from small cleaner Bodianus (~10 cm) to the massive California sheephead (~90 cm), test whether larger species display different color metrics. Semicossyphus pulcher offers a built-in ontogenetic test: juveniles, females, and TP males span the full size range of the species and show dramatic color differences.

  4. Cross-Family Comparison — Compare hypsigenyine color diversity to other labrid clades (Scarinae, Cirrhilabrini, Halichoerinae) to test whether the crusher feeding guild constrains color evolution relative to planktivores or herbivores. The clade also includes one planktivore (Clepticus) that you can compare to its durophagous relatives as an internal contrast (Floeter et al., 2018).
