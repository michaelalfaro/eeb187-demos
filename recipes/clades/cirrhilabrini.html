Cirrhilabrini — Fairy Wrasses and Flasher Wrasses

Natural History

The Cirrhilabrini (fairy wrasses and flasher wrasses) is a tribe within the family Labridae comprising the genera Cirrhilabrus (~60 species), Paracheilinus (~20 species), and several smaller allied genera. This is one of the most rapidly diversifying lineages of coral reef fishes: Tea et al. (2022) used phylogenomic analysis of 991 ultraconserved elements to demonstrate that Cirrhilabrus experienced explosive radiation recently, with many sister species diverging within the past 2–5 million years. This recent diversification, combined with the extraordinary importance of male coloration in species recognition and mate choice, makes Cirrhilabrini one of the most compelling systems in the ocean for studying the role of sexual selection in color diversification.

Cirrhilabrines are planktivorous fishes that hover in loose aggregations above reef substrate, feeding on small zooplankton carried by currents. Most species are associated with reef slopes, rubble zones, or deepwater reef edges. Several lineages have independently colonized mesophotic coral ecosystems (MCEs) at depths of 40–150 m, where light conditions are dramatically different from shallow reefs. These mesophotic specialists often display reduced red and orange coloration and increased emphasis on blue, violet, and UV-reflective patterns — adaptations that align with the spectral properties of ambient light at depth. This depth gradient offers a natural experiment for testing how the light environment constrains color evolution.

The taxonomy of Cirrhilabrini is among the most active frontiers in reef fish systematics. Numerous new species have been described since 2015, often from mesophotic habitats or geographically restricted localities. Cirrhilabrus finifenmaa (Tea et al., 2022) was described from the Maldives based on morphological, molecular, and coloration data — the species had been confused with C. rubrisquamis for decades. Many undescribed species likely remain, particularly in the Indo-Pacific archipelagos and deeper reef habitats. This taxonomic dynamism means that some species in your tree may have been recently split, synonymized, or reclassified since the tree was published.

Reproduction is driven by intense sexual selection. Males are protogynous hermaphrodites that maintain small harems on reef slopes. During courtship, males perform a “nuptial flash” display: they rapidly ascend above the reef, extend their fins to maximum spread, and undergo a dramatic, instantaneous intensification of body coloration — reds become incandescent, blues fluoresce, and pattern elements that are subtle during resting coloration become boldly defined. This flashing display lasts only seconds and may be repeated dozens of times during a courtship bout. The nuptial flash is the most extreme example of rapid color change in any reef fish group and represents the primary axis of sexual selection in this clade.

Color Pattern Diversity

Male cirrhilabrine coloration spans a remarkable spectrum. Cirrhilabrus jordani (Hawaiian flame wrasse) displays brilliant orange-red during nuptial display; C. cyanopleura shows vivid blue and purple with a yellow-green saddle; C. temminckii combines magenta fins with a green-blue body. Paracheilinus flasher wrasses are named for their even more dramatic fin extensions during display, with P. nursalim showing electric blue dorsal fin filaments against a red body.

An extraordinary feature of this clade is the convergent drab coloration of females. Across almost all Cirrhilabrus and Paracheilinus species, females are small (typically 4–6 cm), uniformly pinkish-tan to mauve, with at most a faint lateral stripe or subtle fin tinting. This convergent female coloration is so extreme that females of different species are often impossible to distinguish in the field — or even from photographs. This pattern strongly suggests that female coloration is under stabilizing selection (crypsis from predators), while male coloration is under directional sexual selection (species recognition and mate attraction).

Color convergences across families are less pronounced than in other clades, because cirrhilabrine coloration is largely driven by sexual selection rather than ecological factors. However, the nuptial color intensification phenomenon has parallels in some damselfishes (Pomacentridae) and dottybacks (Pseudochromidae).

Dimorphism and Ontogeny Warnings

Data quality: what to watch for

Extreme sexual dimorphism — use TP males. Like parrotfishes, cirrhilabrines have extreme dimorphism, but the pattern is different. In Scarinae, the issue is that IP and TP look like different species. In Cirrhilabrini, the issue is that females of different species look nearly identical, while males are the highly differentiated phase. Your analysis should use male (TP) images consistently.

How to identify males:

  • Color: Vivid, species-specific patterns with bold reds, blues, purples, greens, or combinations. Complex fin markings, often with elongated or filamentous dorsal rays.
  • Size: Males are generally larger than females within a species (6–10 cm for most Cirrhilabrus).
  • Behavior (in photos): Males are often photographed displaying (fins spread, body angled upward).

How to identify females (to exclude or flag):

  • Color: Uniform pinkish-tan, mauve, or light reddish-brown. Minimal fin coloration. May have a faint lateral stripe.
  • Size: Smaller, more streamlined.
  • If you cannot determine whether an image is a male or female, it is almost certainly a female — males are unmistakable.

Nuptial vs. resting coloration: Male Cirrhilabrus and Paracheilinus show two distinct color states: a muted “resting” coloration and an intensified “nuptial flash” coloration. Most underwater photographs capture resting coloration. Aquarium photographs sometimes capture nuptial display. For consistency, use resting-state male images across all species. If you only have nuptial-display images for some species and resting images for others, your color metrics will be biased.

Identical-looking females are real biology, not a data problem. When you see that your female images for 15 different species look essentially the same, do not assume you have misidentified species. This convergent female coloration is a genuine evolutionary phenomenon and is itself an interesting finding worth discussing in your analysis.

Recent taxonomic changes: Some species in your tree may have been split or renamed since 2022. Cross-check any unfamiliar names against the original Tea et al. (2022) phylogeny and current FishBase/Catalog of Fishes records.

Promising Questions for This Clade

  1. Sexual System and Color Complexity — Cirrhilabrini is the ultimate test case for sexual selection driving color diversification. Is male conspicuousness (measured by pavo metrics like Jc, Jt, or dS) correlated with proxies for mating system intensity? Do species with more elaborate fin extensions also show greater color complexity?

  2. Disparity Through Time — With rapid recent diversification documented by Tea et al. (2022), cirrhilabrines are ideal for DTT analysis. Has male color diversified faster than expected under Brownian motion? Has the rate of color evolution accelerated during the recent species radiation?

  3. Depth and Color — Multiple cirrhilabrine lineages have independently invaded mesophotic reefs. Do mesophotic specialists show predictable shifts in color metrics (e.g., reduced red chroma, increased short-wavelength reflectance) compared to shallow-reef relatives?

  4. Phylogenetic Signal — Given that male coloration is the primary species-recognition signal, do you expect strong or weak phylogenetic signal in male color metrics? If sexual selection drives rapid divergence, signal may be low despite the clade’s youth.
