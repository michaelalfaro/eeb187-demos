Natural History

The Acanthuridae (surgeonfishes, tangs, and unicornfishes) is a family of approximately 86 species in 6 genera, distributed across tropical and subtropical reefs worldwide. The family is characterized by the caudal peduncle spine (or “scalpel”) — a sharp, blade-like structure at the base of the tail that can be erected during defensive or agonistic encounters. In many species, this spine is brightly colored (yellow, orange, or white against a dark body), making it a candidate for aposematic signaling. The caudal scalpel is a genuine weapon: it can inflict deep lacerations on predators, competitors, and careless divers, and its presence distinguishes acanthurids from all other reef fish families. This combination of a real defense with a conspicuous marking makes surgeonfishes uniquely suited for testing the link between weaponry and color evolution.

The three major genera structure acanthurid diversity: Acanthurus (~40 species, typical surgeonfishes), Naso (~20 species, unicornfishes), and Ctenochaetus (~10 species, bristletooth surgeonfishes). These genera occupy distinct feeding niches: most Acanthurus are grazing herbivores that crop algal turfs from reef substrate; Ctenochaetus species are detritivores that use comb-like teeth to sweep fine organic particles from surfaces; and Naso includes both grazers and open-water planktivores that form large midwater aggregations. The smaller genera Zebrasoma (tangs), Paracanthurus, and Prionurus round out the family. This diversity of feeding guilds within a single family provides a strong ecological axis for color-pattern analysis.

Surgeonfishes are broadcast spawners, typically releasing gametes during pair or group spawning rushes at reef edges during outgoing tides. Unlike labrids, most acanthurids are gonochoristic — they do not change sex — and sexual dichromatism is weak to absent in most species. Some species show transient courtship color changes: males of Acanthurus nigricans temporarily pale their body during spawning rushes, and A. lineatus males intensify the contrast of their facial stripes during territorial displays. These context-dependent color changes, while not as dramatic as labrid nuptial displays, add complexity to the interpretation of color metrics from static photographs.

This is the walkthrough dataset for the course. Frédérich et al. (2026) used Acanthuridae as one of six focal families in their global analysis of color pattern evolution in reef fishes, coding 30 pattern types across 918 species from six families. Your group’s analysis will follow a similar approach but with pavo-derived quantitative metrics.

Color Pattern Diversity

Acanthurid coloration ranges from uniform and relatively drab to strikingly bold. At one extreme, Ctenochaetus strigosus is uniformly brown with fine dark streaks, while at the other, Acanthurus lineatus displays brilliant alternating blue-and-orange horizontal stripes. Zebrasoma flavescens (the yellow tang) is uniformly bright yellow — one of the most recognizable reef fish in the world — while its congener Z. veliferum has complex brown-and-yellow vertical barring with extended dorsal and anal fins.

Unicornfishes (Naso) present a distinctive morphology: several species develop a prominent rostral horn and display muted blue-gray or olive body coloration with brightly colored caudal peduncle spines (paired in Naso, unlike the single retractable scalpel in Acanthurus). The coloration of the scalpel region is often the most conspicuous element of the fish and may serve a dual function: warning potential aggressors and facilitating species recognition in mixed-species feeding aggregations.

One of the most remarkable color stories in Acanthuridae involves Batesian mimicry. Juvenile Acanthurus pyroferus (the mimic surgeonfish) adopt the color pattern of locally common pygmy angelfishes (Centropyge spp.), including C. vrolikii, C. flavissima, C. heraldi, and C. eibli, depending on which model species is present in their habitat. As adults, A. pyroferus transitions to a uniform chocolate-brown coloration, losing all resemblance to its angelfish models. This ontogenetic mimicry system — mimicking different models in different geographic locations — is one of the most sophisticated examples of color-based deception on coral reefs.

Several species also show rapid context-dependent color change. Paracanthurus hepatus (blue tang) can pale its blue coloration dramatically when stressed, and several Acanthurus species temporarily display a pale bar or spot pattern during cleaning interactions or submissive encounters.

Dimorphism and Ontogeny Warnings

Data quality: what to watch for

Weak dichromatism — simpler data curation. Unlike labrids and scarines, most acanthurids show little or no sexual dimorphism in coloration. Males and females of the same species look essentially identical in photographs. This means a single representative image per species is generally adequate, and you do not need to worry about IP/TP confusion.

Context-dependent color change: Some species can temporarily alter their color in response to stress, social context, or cleaning interactions. Images taken of stressed fish (netted, recently captured, or in poor aquarium conditions) may show washed-out or abnormally pale coloration that does not represent the species’ typical appearance. When curating images:

  • Prefer photographs of undisturbed fish in natural reef settings.
  • Avoid images where the fish appears unusually pale, mottled, or shows a “fright pattern.”
  • If you have multiple images of the same species, select the one that best represents the species’ typical daytime reef coloration.

Juvenile mimicry in A. pyroferus: If your image set includes juvenile A. pyroferus, they will look like Centropyge angelfishes, not surgeonfishes. Use only adult images for this species (uniform chocolate-brown with a yellow eye-patch).

Ontogeny in other species: Most acanthurid juveniles resemble adults in pattern, though colors may be less saturated. The “acronurus” larval stage (transparent, disc-shaped, pelagic) will not appear in your image set. Juvenile Naso species lack the rostral horn and caudal spine development of adults but share similar body coloration.

Species identification: Several Acanthurus species are superficially similar (e.g., A. nigricans vs. A. achilles vs. A. japonicus). Pay attention to caudal spine coloration and facial markings for accurate identification.

Promising Questions for This Clade

  1. Functional Hypothesis Testing — Surgeonfishes have a genuine weapon (the caudal scalpel) that makes aposematism biologically plausible. Test whether species with more conspicuously colored scalpels (bright yellow/orange against dark body) show higher overall body conspicuousness, as predicted by honest signaling of defensive capability.

  2. Diet and Conspicuousness — With three distinct feeding guilds (grazing, detritivory, planktivory), acanthurids offer a clean test of the prediction that feeding ecology shapes color evolution. Are planktivorous species (which are exposed in the water column) more or less conspicuous than benthic grazers?

  3. Cross-Family Comparison — As the walkthrough dataset, your pavo metrics can be directly compared to other clades. How does the overall color diversity (morphospace volume) of Acanthuridae compare to similarly sized labrid clades?

  4. Discrete Trait Mapping — Map mimicry (juvenile A. pyroferus mimicking Centropyge), scalpel coloration (bright vs. cryptic), and the presence of horizontal striping across the phylogeny to test how many times each trait has evolved.

  5. Latitude Gradient — Several acanthurid species extend into subtropical or warm-temperate waters. Test whether higher-latitude species show reduced conspicuousness compared to tropical congeners.
