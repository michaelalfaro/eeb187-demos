Taxonomy and Phylogenetics

The Acanthuridae comprise approximately 86 extant species in six genera, traditionally arranged in three subfamilies: Acanthurinae (Acanthurus, Ctenochaetus, Zebrasoma, Paracanthurus), Nasinae (Naso), and Prionurinae (Prionurus). The earliest comprehensive molecular phylogeny of the family (Sorenson et al., 2013) used multi-locus nuclear and mitochondrial data to produce a time-calibrated tree, recovering Nasinae and Prionurinae as successively basal to a well-supported Acanthurinae crown clade. Within Acanthurinae, Ctenochaetus nests inside a paraphyletic Acanthurus, and Zebrasoma plus Paracanthurus form a distinct lineage. More recent phylogenomic analyses of acanthuriform fishes (Brownstein et al., 2025) place the origin of crown Acanthuridae in the Early Miocene, with most extant generic diversity arising during a burst of cladogenesis between approximately 20 and 10 Mya that coincides with the expansion of modern Indo-Pacific reef habitat. Ludt et al. (2019) used mitogenomic data to resolve the temperate Prionurus clade, confirming its early divergence from the tropical acanthurine lineages.

Your pruned tree contains 63 species drawn from across all three subfamilies. The outgroup taxa Zanclus cornutus (Moorish idol) and Luvarus imperialis (luvar) — both sister or near-sister to crown Acanthuridae in most analyses (Sorenson et al., 2013) — have been removed for this exercise, so every tip in your tree represents a true acanthurid. Acanthurus and Naso together account for roughly two-thirds of the family’s species richness and dominate the tree.

Natural History

Acanthurids are circumglobally distributed across tropical and subtropical reefs, with their highest species richness in the Indo-Australian Archipelago and progressively reduced diversity across the Pacific and into the Atlantic (Bellwood et al., 2019). The family’s signature morphological feature is the caudal peduncle spine (“scalpel”) — a sharp, blade-like structure that can inflict deep lacerations on competitors and predators. In Acanthurus, Ctenochaetus, Zebrasoma, and Paracanthurus the spine is single and retractable into a sheath; in Naso it is replaced by one or two fixed keeled plates on each side of the peduncle; in Prionurus a row of fixed bony plates lines the peduncle.

Surgeonfishes are central to coral-reef ecosystem function. Most species are herbivores or detritivores that crop algal turfs, remove sediment from reef substrate, and graze on detrital aggregates (Choat et al., 2012). This grazing pressure regulates benthic community composition and helps maintain coral-dominated rather than macroalgae-dominated reef states. Recent comparative work (Mihalitsis et al., 2025) shows that acanthurid feeding morphology has diversified repeatedly along axes of substrate scraping, water-column planktivory, and selective browsing, with each axis associated with characteristic head and jaw shapes. Ctenochaetus uses comb-like teeth to sweep fine detrital particles from coral rubble, and these particles are processed by a specialized gut microbiome that ferments algal polysaccharides (Ngugi et al., 2017). Naso includes both benthic grazers (e.g., N. unicornis) and large midwater planktivores (e.g., N. hexacanthus, N. vlamingii) that form aggregations off reef edges.

Reproduction is broadcast spawning in pairs or groups, typically at reef edges during outgoing tides. Unlike labrids and pomacanthids, acanthurids are gonochoristic and do not change sex. Larvae pass through a distinctive transparent, disc-shaped pelagic “acronurus” stage before settling onto reefs. Phylogeographic studies of Acanthurus species have documented strong genetic connectivity across the Indo-Pacific, consistent with a long pelagic larval duration and frequent long-distance dispersal (Gaither et al., 2015).

Visual Ecology and Signaling

Acanthurids inhabit shallow, well-lit reef environments where the full visible spectrum is available, and several lines of evidence indicate that they exploit this broad spectral range. Losey et al. (2003) measured ocular media transmittance in 195 species of Hawaiian reef fishes and showed that many acanthurids transmit substantial ultraviolet (UV) light to the retina — a prerequisite for UV vision. Marshall (2000) reviewed the visual ecology of coral reef fishes and argued that the steep spectral gradient of shallow water creates conditions in which both long-wavelength (orange, red) and short-wavelength (blue, violet, UV) signals can convey information over short distances, even though they attenuate rapidly with depth. Siebeck et al. (2014) used behavioral assays and microspectrophotometry to demonstrate that reef fishes can extract luminance and chromatic information across at least four spectral channels, supporting a tetrachromatic interpretation of visual ecology in many shallow reef taxa. A subsequent comparative review (Siebeck et al., 2018) placed surgeonfishes among the reef-fish families with the broadest spectral sensitivities documented to date.

The functional significance of UV sensitivity in acanthurids remains an active research area, but two non-exclusive hypotheses dominate the literature. First, UV reflectance from body markings and from the caudal-peduncle region may function as a private signaling channel that is highly visible to conspecifics at close range but invisible to many large predators whose ocular media block UV (Siebeck & Marshall, 2000; Siebeck et al., 2018). Second, UV signals attenuate rapidly with distance, making them effective for short-range species recognition during the dense aggregating and schooling behavior that characterizes many surgeonfish species. The combination of UV-transmitting ocular media, conspicuous body markings, and tight social aggregations places acanthurids among the most promising clades for testing private-channel hypotheses in reef fishes.

Color Pattern Diversity

Acanthurid color patterns span a remarkable range. At one extreme, Ctenochaetus strigosus is uniformly drab brown with fine dark streaks; at the other, Acanthurus lineatus displays brilliant alternating blue and orange horizontal stripes. Zebrasoma flavescens is uniformly bright yellow — one of the most recognizable fishes in the world — while its congener Z. veliferum shows complex brown-and-yellow vertical barring set off by extended median fins. Paracanthurus hepatus (palette surgeonfish) carries a bold “palette” of cobalt blue, black, and yellow that has become iconic in the popular imagination. Naso species generally show muted blue-gray or olive body coloration set against vividly colored caudal-peduncle keels (yellow, orange, or electric blue) and, in some species, ornate facial markings or rostral horns.

Frédérich et al. (2026) coded 30 discrete pattern categories across 918 species in six reef-fish families, including Acanthuridae, and found that pattern diversity scales positively with species richness and shows pronounced lability across the family. Their analyses recover repeated independent origins of stripes, bars, spots, and large color blocks within Acanthuridae, with no single pattern type strongly conserved at the subfamily level. Hemingson et al. (2018) similarly documented rapid color-pattern divergence among sympatric reef fishes and identified surgeonfishes as a group with above-average pattern turnover relative to phylogenetic age. The earlier review by Frédérich et al. (2019) frames these patterns within the broader “magic trait” literature, arguing that reef-fish color patterns can simultaneously mediate species recognition, mate choice, and ecological signaling — a combination that may accelerate diversification.

Pattern convergences across families are notable. The bold vertical barring of Zebrasoma veliferum parallels patterns in pomacanthids and chaetodontids that share its reef-edge habitat. Conversely, the uniform bright yellow of Z. flavescens converges on the lemonpeel angelfish (Centropyge flavissima) at similar depths and latitudes — a convergence that may reflect shared signaling environments rather than common ancestry (Floeter et al., 2018).

Sexual Dimorphism, Ontogeny, and Polymorphism

Sexual dichromatism is weak or absent in most acanthurid species, and males and females typically appear essentially identical in static photographs. This contrasts sharply with labrids and pomacanthids and simplifies data curation for comparative analysis. Several species do show transient context-dependent color change, however. Males of Acanthurus nigricans pale dramatically during spawning rushes, and territorial A. lineatus males intensify the contrast of their facial stripes during agonistic displays. Paracanthurus hepatus can fade its blue coloration when stressed or at night. These transient states are not stable diagnostic traits but can introduce noise into pavo-derived metrics if a substantial fraction of images captured fish in atypical condition.

Ontogenetic color change in Acanthuridae is generally subtle compared with the extreme juvenile-to-adult transformations seen in Pomacanthus, with one striking exception: juvenile Acanthurus pyroferus exhibit geographic mimicry of locally abundant Centropyge pygmy angelfishes, adopting yellow (C. flavissima-like), brown (C. vrolikii-like), or pale-yellow (C. heraldi-like) coloration depending on which model species predominates in their habitat (Eagle & Jones, 2004; Rainey, 2009). Adults transition to a uniform chocolate-brown body with a yellow eye patch, losing all resemblance to the angelfish models. This system is one of the best-documented cases of ontogenetic, geographically variable mimicry in vertebrates and remains an active area of research on the developmental basis of color-pattern lability. Juvenile Naso species lack the rostral horn and elaborated caudal armature of adults but generally share body coloration with conspecific adults.

Caudal-Spine Aposematism: A Clade-Specific Deep Dive

The caudal scalpel of Acanthuridae is a genuine weapon, and its often-conspicuous coloration has long invited interpretation as an aposematic signal. In Acanthurus achilles and several other Acanthurus species the spine sheath is outlined in bright orange or red against a dark body; in Naso the keeled peduncle plates are frequently bordered with yellow or orange; in Prionurus the row of bony plates is darker than the surrounding skin and bordered by a pale margin. These color-on-weapon combinations satisfy the basic structural prediction of aposematism: a conspicuous signal placed directly on a defensive structure (Marshall, 2000).

Formal tests of aposematism in acanthurids remain limited, but the comparative ingredients are now in place. Frédérich et al. (2026) provide pattern-coded data for the family; visual-ecology studies (Losey et al., 2003; Siebeck et al., 2018) document the spectral sensitivity of likely receivers; and phylogenetic comparative methods can readily map caudal-region conspicuousness against measures of overall body conspicuousness, body size, depth, and group living. Hemingson et al. (2018) and Frédérich et al. (2019) emphasize that color signals in reef fishes can carry multiple non-exclusive functions (species recognition, social signaling, defense), so single-function explanations should be tested rather than assumed. A pavo-based analysis of contrast between the caudal-spine region and the adjacent flank — mapped onto the Sorenson et al. (2013) phylogeny — would constitute a tractable and publishable test of the aposematism hypothesis.

Data Quality Warnings

Data quality: what to watch for

Weak dichromatism — simpler data curation. Most acanthurids show little or no sexual dimorphism in coloration, so a single representative image per species is generally adequate and you do not need to track initial-phase vs. terminal-phase morphs as you would in labrids.

Context-dependent color change. Some species can temporarily alter body color in response to stress, social context, or cleaning interactions. Images of recently netted, captive, or stressed fish may show washed-out or “fright pattern” coloration that does not represent the species’ typical reef appearance. When curating images:

  • Prefer photographs of undisturbed fish in natural reef settings.
  • Avoid images where the fish appears unusually pale, mottled, or blotchy.
  • If multiple images are available, select the one that best represents typical daytime reef coloration.

Juvenile mimicry in A. pyroferus. Juveniles of this species mimic Centropyge pygmy angelfishes and will look nothing like adult A. pyroferus. Use only adult images (uniform chocolate-brown body with a yellow eye-patch). The same precaution applies to any locally documented mimic morphs.

Outgroup taxa already removed. Your tree has been pruned so that Zanclus cornutus and Luvarus imperialis do not appear. If you encounter any non-acanthurid tips during your analysis, flag them and check the tree before proceeding.

Species identification. Several Acanthurus species are superficially similar (e.g., A. nigricans, A. achilles, A. japonicus). Pay close attention to caudal-spine coloration and facial markings when identifying images. Misidentification at the species level will propagate directly into your pavo metrics.

UV reflectance is invisible to standard cameras. Any UV-mediated signals discussed in the literature will not be captured by your RGB images. Treat conclusions about overall conspicuousness as conservative: a species that looks drab to humans may carry strong UV signals that are highly visible to conspecifics.

Promising Questions for This Clade

  1. Functional Hypothesis Testing — Surgeonfishes have a genuine weapon (the caudal scalpel) that makes aposematism biologically plausible. Test whether species with more conspicuously colored scalpel regions show higher overall body conspicuousness, as predicted by honest signaling of defensive capability.

  2. Diet and Conspicuousness — With three distinct feeding guilds (grazing, detritivory, planktivory), acanthurids offer a clean test of the prediction that feeding ecology shapes color evolution. Are planktivorous species (which are exposed in the water column) more or less conspicuous than benthic grazers?

  3. Cross-Family Comparison — As the walkthrough dataset, your pavo metrics can be directly compared to other clades. How does the overall color diversity (morphospace volume) of Acanthuridae compare to similarly sized labrid or pomacanthid clades?

  4. Discrete Trait Mapping — Map mimicry (juvenile A. pyroferus mimicking Centropyge), scalpel coloration (bright vs. cryptic), and the presence of horizontal striping across the phylogeny to test how many times each trait has evolved.

  5. Latitude Gradient — Several acanthurid species, especially in Prionurus and some Naso, extend into subtropical or warm-temperate waters. Test whether higher-latitude species show reduced conspicuousness compared to tropical congeners.
