Taxonomy and Phylogenetics

Parrotfishes have one of the most unsettled taxonomic histories of any reef fish group. Traditionally treated as the family Scaridae, molecular phylogenetic work over the past two decades has shown unambiguously that parrotfishes are nested deep inside the wrasse family Labridae (Streelman & Karl, 1997; Westneat & Alfaro, 2005). The modern convention is to treat them as the subfamily Scarinae within Labridae, although many ecological and fisheries papers still use Scaridae. Whatever the rank, the clade comprises roughly 100 living species in ten genera: Scarus (~52 species), Chlorurus (~18), Sparisoma (~15), and the smaller genera Cetoscarus, Bolbometopon, Hipposcarus, Cryptotomus, Calotomus, Leptoscarus, and Nicholsina (Choat et al., 2012). Your tree includes 78 of these.

Molecular work by Streelman, Alfaro & Westneat (2002) overturned the morphology-based phylogeny of Bellwood (1994) and recovered a deep split between a “seagrass clade” (Cryptotomus, Nicholsina, Leptoscarus, Calotomus, and most Sparisoma) and a “reef clade” (Bolbometopon, Cetoscarus, Hipposcarus, Chlorurus, and Scarus) that contains roughly 80 percent of described species. The expanded multilocus analysis of Streelman, Smith & Fessler (2008) confirmed this two-clade topology and, using fossil-calibrated codon-rate models, dated the parrotfish crown to ~22 Ma in the Miocene with most species-level divergence in Scarus and Chlorurus concentrated in the last 5 Ma. The most comprehensive species-level treatment to date is Choat et al. (2012), who reconstructed relationships among 61 of the ~70 Scarus + Chlorurus species and recovered 15 well-supported clades whose origins fell in the mid-Pliocene to Pleistocene. They found that sympatric sister-species pairs were more divergent in morphology, ecology, and dichromatic color pattern than allopatric pairs — suggesting that ecological and signal divergence have actively driven speciation in this clade rather than simply being passive byproducts of allopatry.

Natural History

Parrotfishes are circumtropical and occupy nearly every shallow tropical hard-bottom and seagrass habitat between roughly 1 and 40 m depth. Their biogeography is structured by a deep Atlantic vs. Indo-Pacific split: Sparisoma (and the seagrass-associated Cryptotomus and Nicholsina) dominates Caribbean and tropical Atlantic reefs, while Scarus and Chlorurus dominate the Indo-Pacific, where they are among the most abundant non-cryptic fishes on any given reef (Choat et al., 2019). A few species (most notably Scarus ghobban and S. rubroviolaceus) range broadly across the Indo-Pacific, and the Tropical Eastern Pacific holds an interesting four-species Scarus assemblage that includes the hybrid-origin S. compressus (Robertson, Carlon & Barron, 2021).

Almost all parrotfishes are herbivores or detritivores, but their feeding ecology subdivides into functional groups that map onto jaw morphology and body size. Scrapers (most Scarus, small Sparisoma) use their fused beak-like oral jaws to remove a thin layer of algal turf, sediment, and the microbial epilithic and endolithic film from coral and rock surfaces. Excavators (large Chlorurus, Bolbometopon muricatum) take much deeper bites, gouging chunks of carbonate framework with each pass. Browsers (several Sparisoma, Calotomus, Leptoscarus) preferentially target fleshy macroalgae and seagrasses. Recent isotopic and microscopy work (e.g., Bellwood et al., 2012) suggests that even scraping and excavating species are largely targeting protein-rich endolithic cyanobacteria rather than the algal turf per se — a reinterpretation that has reshaped how we think about parrotfish “herbivory.” Whatever the precise food, the ecological consequence is dramatic: parrotfishes are the dominant external bioeroders on most coral reefs worldwide, accounting for 60 to >90 percent of all measured bioerosion in cross-shelf and global comparisons, and contributing several tons of carbonate sediment per hectare per year (Bellwood et al., 2012; Mumby, Bozec & O’Farrell, 2016). The bumphead parrotfish Bolbometopon muricatum, the largest scarine at ~130 cm and 46 kg, alone can ingest more than five tonnes of structural carbonate per year per individual.

Social organization is dominated by protogynous hermaphroditism, almost always in a diandric form: most individuals begin life as females (or as drab “initial phase,” IP, primary males) and the largest fish ultimately transition into vivid “terminal phase” (TP) males that defend territories or harems and dominate spawning (Robertson & Warner, 1978). Mating systems range from haremic with site-attached TP males (typical of many Scarus) to group spawning and lek-like aggregations (typical of some Caribbean Sparisoma), and these social systems strongly influence both body-size distributions and the size at sex change (Choat & Taylor, 2014). One of the most distinctive natural-history traits in the clade is the nocturnal mucous cocoon that parrotfishes (and some wrasses) spin around themselves before sleeping in reef crevices: Grutter et al. (2010) showed experimentally that these cocoons are not principally an antipredator barrier but rather a “mosquito net” that blocks bloodfeeding gnathiid isopods, at a cost of about 2.5 percent of the fish’s daily energy budget.

Visual Ecology and Signaling

Parrotfishes live in the brightest, most spectrally rich light environment available to any reef fish: shallow (1–15 m) reef flats and crests bathed in broadband downwelling sunlight that retains substantial energy from ultraviolet through far-red. This light environment imposes very different selective pressures on visual systems than the deeper, blue-shifted habitats of pomacentrids or anthiines, and it permits color signals across the full visible spectrum. Direct visual-pigment data are sparse for parrotfishes themselves, but the broader labrid visual literature provides strong context. Stieb, Cortesi et al. (2017) showed that damselfishes — which share parrotfish habitats — retain all five major teleost cone opsin classes (SWS1 in the UV, SWS2 in the blue, two RH2 paralogs in the green, and LWS in the red) and that opsin sequence and expression evolve under strong positive selection associated with feeding ecology and body color. A follow-up multi-family study by Stieb, Cortesi et al. (2023) included the parrotfish Chlorurus sordidus and found that herbivorous reef fishes upregulate the long-wavelength-sensitive (LWS) opsin relative to zooplanktivores, and that high LWS expression correlates with possession of orange/red body coloration — exactly the trait state typical of many parrotfish IP individuals and several Caribbean Sparisoma. This pattern is consistent with the “sensory bias” prediction that long-wavelength sensitivity evolved first for detecting algae against the reef substrate and was subsequently co-opted for intraspecific signaling.

For your group’s purposes, the practical implication is that parrotfishes very likely have access to the full reef color gamut and can in principle discriminate the subtle hue and saturation differences that distinguish closely related TP males. Visual modeling against a labrid or damselfish receiver should be defensible.

Color Pattern Diversity

Few reef fish clades approach the parrotfishes in raw color-pattern diversity, and almost none match them in within-species polymorphism. Indo-Pacific TP Scarus and Chlorurus males display some of the most saturated greens, teals, and electric blues found in any vertebrate: Scarus quoyi TP males have a deep blue-green body and a magenta lower jaw; Chlorurus microrhinos TP males are bright emerald with a prominent cephalic hump and pink facial bars; S. ghobban TP males have an iridescent yellow-and-blue scale pattern. Caribbean Sparisoma tend toward a different color space dominated by red-browns, oranges, and dark greens, with crescent-shaped tail markings (e.g., S. viride) and bicolored tails (S. aurofrenatum). Across the family there is a strong axis of variation between species with high-contrast, intricately patterned TP faces (Scarus, Chlorurus) and species with more uniform body coloration (Bolbometopon, Cetoscarus).

Streelman, Smith & Fessler (2008) attempted to map color characters onto a molecular phylogeny of Scarus and Chlorurus and found that color patterns evolve rapidly and recurrently, with little phylogenetic conservatism at the level of specific facial markings. This is one of the reasons that morphology-based parrotfish taxonomy proved so unreliable: color characters that appear diagnostically distinct turn out to have evolved independently many times, and species that look very similar can be only distantly related within Scarus. Choat et al. (2012) further showed that sympatric sister species are more divergent in color than allopatric ones, consistent with a role for visual signaling in reinforcing reproductive isolation where parrotfishes overlap.

Sexual Dimorphism, Ontogeny, and Polymorphism

The Scarinae are the textbook example of extreme sexual dichromatism in reef fishes. In most species, IP individuals (juveniles, females, and primary males) and TP individuals (terminal-phase, sex-changed or large primary males) differ so drastically in coloration that nineteenth- and early twentieth-century ichthyologists routinely described them as separate species (Robertson & Warner, 1978; Choat et al., 2012). Robertson and Warner’s (1978) monograph on ten Caribbean parrotfishes remains the foundational treatment: they documented that protogynous sex change in the Scaridae is typically diandric — some males develop directly from juveniles as drab primary males (IP males) while others develop first as females and later transform into vivid TP males — and that the IP and TP color phases correspond to fundamentally different mating tactics (sneaking and group spawning by IP males vs. territorial defense and pair spawning by TP males).

Ontogeny adds a third pattern layer: juveniles of many Scarus are uniformly dark with a pale lateral stripe, becoming IP-like only after several months. Cetoscarus bicolor juveniles are white with a black-edged orange spot and look nothing like either adult phase. Some species also harbor intraspecific color polymorphism within a single sex-and-phase class: Sparisoma rubripinne has both gonochoristic and protogynous populations and IP males with multiple distinct color morphs; Scarus iseri juveniles include both a “striped” and a “bicolored” morph. Hybridization adds yet more variation in regions of sympatry: Robertson et al. (2021) showed that nearly 90 percent of Scarus compressus individuals in the Tropical Eastern Pacific are F1 hybrids between S. perrico and either S. ghobban or S. rubroviolaceus, producing intermediate IP and TP phenotypes that have confused taxonomists since the species was first described.

Initial-Phase / Terminal-Phase Color Evolution

If your group wants to anchor its project around a single big question, IP/TP color evolution is the obvious choice for Scarinae. The system has several features that make it analytically tractable. First, the trait is genuinely binary at the level of an individual photograph: in nearly every species, a fish either bears the IP color pattern or the TP color pattern, with relatively few transitional individuals (Robertson & Warner, 1978). Second, the IP-to-TP transformation is repeatable: it happens during a discrete sex-change event in protogynous hermaphrodites, and the corresponding color change is hormonally regulated and runs to completion over days to weeks. Third, the magnitude of IP-TP color difference is itself a quantitative trait that varies dramatically across species — from the radical difference in Scarus vetula or S. quoyi to the relatively subtle difference in Cryptotomus roseus or Bolbometopon muricatum — and that variation can be mapped onto the Choat et al. (2012) phylogeny.

Several biologically interesting predictions follow. Larger TP-male territories and more intense male-male competition should select for more extreme TP coloration (Choat & Taylor, 2014, who showed that size at sex change varies systematically with reef exposure and mating-system intensity). Species with diandric mating systems — where IP males also spawn and benefit from female mimicry — should retain more drab and female-like IP coloration to support sneak-spawning, while species with strictly protogynous (monandric) systems can let IP color drift more freely (Robertson & Warner, 1978). Recent molecular and visual-modeling work in damselfishes (Stieb et al., 2023) suggests that the evolution of long-wavelength visual sensitivity may have preceded and enabled the elaboration of red/orange social signals — a hypothesis that could be tested directly in parrotfishes given the strong red/orange component of many Sparisoma TP patterns and the basal position of Sparisoma in the broader scarine phylogeny.

Data Quality Warnings

Data quality: read this section before you start

The IP/TP problem is the single largest data-quality risk in this entire course. Initial-phase and terminal-phase parrotfishes look like entirely different species in nearly every member of your tree. If you mix IP and TP photographs even casually — one IP Scarus vetula alongside a TP Scarus iseri, for example — your color metrics will be dominated by phase variation rather than species variation and your phylogenetic comparative analyses will be uninterpretable. Pick one phase and stick with it for every species. We recommend TP males because (a) they are the most photographed phase in field guides and online image repositories, (b) they show the greatest interspecific color variation (giving you signal to work with), and (c) they represent the reproductively mature, fully-developed adult phenotype that comparative methods are usually trying to capture.

How to identify TP males in photographs:

  • Vivid blues, greens, and teals, with complex facial markings (lines, swirls, spots around the eye and jaw).
  • Often larger than IP individuals; sometimes with a developed cephalic hump (Chlorurus, Bolbometopon, Cetoscarus).
  • Often photographed solo or patrolling, rather than in mixed groups.

How to identify IP individuals to exclude:

  • Drab red-brown, olive, gray, or muted maroon. Often blotchy or uniformly washed-out.
  • Smaller, more streamlined, no cephalic hump.
  • Often in loose feeding groups.

Juveniles: Many Scarus juveniles are uniformly dark with a pale stripe; Cetoscarus bicolor juveniles are white with a black-edged orange spot. Exclude juveniles entirely.

Species with weak or unusual dichromatism (handle with care):

  • Cryptotomus roseus: both phases translucent pink; will be a color-space outlier.
  • Bolbometopon muricatum: both phases relatively uniform olive-gray; TP identified by size and forehead bump rather than color.
  • Sparisoma generally: IP/TP difference is real but more muted than in Indo-Pacific Scarus.

Historical taxonomic confusion: Robertson & Warner (1978) documented that many nineteenth-century parrotfish “species” turned out to be IP or juvenile phases of already-named species. Older field guides and museum lots may still carry these misidentifications. If a name in your image set returns very few hits or images that look inconsistent, cross-check against FishBase synonymy before including it.

Hybrids: Scarus compressus in the Tropical Eastern Pacific is largely a hybrid population (Robertson et al., 2021) with intermediate IP and TP phenotypes. If your tree includes S. compressus, treat it cautiously or exclude it.

Consistency rule: For every species, verify that you are using a TP male image. If you cannot find a confirmed TP male photograph for a given species, drop that species rather than substitute an IP image.

Promising Questions for This Clade

  1. Sexual System and Color Complexity — Scarinae is arguably the best clade in the entire student tree for testing whether protogynous hermaphroditism drives the evolution of extreme dichromatism. Compare TP-male color complexity across species and ask whether the magnitude of IP–TP color difference correlates with mating-system intensity (haremic territoriality vs. group spawning).

  2. Body Size and Color Complexity — With a >10-fold size range from Cryptotomus (~12 cm) to Bolbometopon (~130 cm), parrotfishes offer a strong gradient for testing whether larger species can “afford” more complex color patterns (greater visual target area, reduced predation risk).

  3. Biogeographic Color Comparison — The Atlantic (Sparisoma) vs. Indo-Pacific (Scarus, Chlorurus) split is one of the cleanest biogeographic contrasts in any reef fish family. Do Indo-Pacific parrotfishes occupy a broader region of color space? Has the Atlantic lineage been more constrained?

  4. Diet and Conspicuousness — Scrapers, excavators, and browsers differ in feeding posture, reef-surface exposure time, and the chromatic background against which they feed. Do excavators (which spend more time head-down on bright carbonate) differ in TP color or contrast from scrapers (more often in motion against algal turf)?
