Scarinae — Parrotfishes

Natural History

Parrotfishes (subfamily Scarinae) are a clade of approximately 100 species nested within the family Labridae. Their evolutionary history has been shaped by two defining innovations: the fused beak-like oral jaws that give them their common name, and the pharyngeal mill — a set of interlocking pharyngeal jaw plates that grind ingested reef substrate into fine sedite. These feeding adaptations have made parrotfishes the dominant agents of bioerosion on coral reefs worldwide, contributing tons of sediment per hectare per year to reef sand budgets. The phylogenetic position of Scarinae within Labridae was established by molecular data (Streelman et al., 2002; Westneat & Alfaro, 2005) and confirmed by the comprehensive phylogenomic analysis of Brownstein et al. (2025), which placed the parrotfish radiation within the broader context of labrid diversification during the Miocene.

Two major functional groups structure parrotfish feeding ecology: scrapers (e.g., Scarus, most Sparisoma) that remove a thin layer of substrate with their beaks, and excavators (e.g., Chlorurus, Bolbometopon) that gouge deep chunks from the reef framework. Scraping vs. excavating has evolved recurrently across the scarinine phylogeny and correlates with jaw morphology, body size, and habitat use (Bellwood, 1994; Streelman et al., 2002). This functional divergence provides a strong ecological axis for color-pattern analysis.

A deep biogeographic split divides the parrotfishes: the Atlantic genus Sparisoma (~15 species) diverged early from the Indo-Pacific radiation that includes Scarus (~50 species), Chlorurus (~17 species), and Bolbometopon (1 species). Atlantic Sparisoma tend to retain more of the ancestral red-brown coloration, while Indo-Pacific Scarus and Chlorurus have diversified into vivid blues, greens, and complex multicolor patterns. This Atlantic vs. Indo-Pacific split is one of the cleanest natural experiments in reef fish color evolution.

The reproductive biology of parrotfishes is dominated by protogynous hermaphroditism, typically in a diandric system: some individuals begin life as females and later undergo sex change to become terminal-phase (TP) males, while others are born as primary males that initially resemble females (initial phase, IP). Sex change is accompanied by a dramatic color transformation — arguably the most extreme in any vertebrate group. This creates the IP/TP dimorphism that defines the data challenges (and opportunities) for your group.

Color Pattern Diversity

Parrotfish color diversity is overwhelmingly structured by the IP/TP dimorphism. Initial-phase individuals (females and primary males) are typically drab: muted red-brown, olive, or gray, often with irregular darker blotching. Terminal-phase males, by contrast, are spectacularly colored: vivid blue-green heads, complex facial patterns with pink, orange, or teal markings, and body coloration ranging from emerald to turquoise to deep blue. The contrast between IP and TP in a single species can be so extreme that early taxonomists routinely described them as separate species — Scarus vetula and S. guacamaia IP and TP forms were classified under different names for decades.

Within the TP color space, there is substantial interspecific variation. Scarus quoyi TP males are deep blue-green with a pink lower jaw; Chlorurus microrhinos TPs are bright green with a prominent cephalic hump; Bolbometopon muricatum TPs are olive-gray with a massive forehead bump. Sparisoma viride shows a more moderate dimorphism, with TPs displaying dark green bodies and crescent-shaped tail markings, and IPs showing red-brown bodies with a white spot at the caudal peduncle.

Size variation is also remarkable: the cryptic, cigar-shaped Cryptotomus roseus reaches only ~12 cm total length, while the bumphead parrotfish Bolbometopon muricatum exceeds 130 cm and 46 kg — a body size range of more than 10-fold within a single subfamily.

Dimorphism and Ontogeny Warnings

Data quality: THIS IS YOUR BIGGEST CHALLENGE

IP/TP dimorphism — use TP males consistently. This is the single most important data-quality issue for your group. Initial-phase and terminal-phase parrotfishes look like entirely different species. If you mix IP and TP images in your pavo analysis, your color metrics will be meaningless. Use only TP (terminal-phase) male images for your comparative analysis.

How to identify TP males:

  • Color: Vivid blues, greens, teals, with complex facial markings (lines, swirls, spots around the eye and jaw).
  • Body shape: In many species, TP males are larger and may develop a cephalic hump (especially Chlorurus, Bolbometopon).
  • Behavior (in photos): TP males are often photographed solo or patrolling a territory, while IP individuals often appear in loose groups.

How to identify IP individuals (to exclude):

  • Color: Drab red-brown, olive, gray, or muted maroon. May have irregular blotches or a more uniform, washed-out appearance.
  • Body shape: Smaller, no cephalic hump, more streamlined.

Juveniles: Very young parrotfish may show yet another color pattern. Juvenile Scarus are often uniformly dark brown or gray, sometimes with a pale stripe. In labrids including scarines, females and juveniles often retain the juvenile color morph. You will encounter some juvenile images in your set — exclude them.

Species-specific notes:

  • Sparisoma species: IP/TP dimorphism is less extreme than in Scarus/Chlorurus. IPs still tend toward red-brown, TPs toward green/blue, but the difference is subtler.
  • Cryptotomus roseus: Minimal dimorphism; both phases are translucent pink. This species will be an outlier in your color space.
  • Bolbometopon muricatum: Adults are relatively uniform olive-gray regardless of phase. TP males are identified by their massive size and prominent forehead bump rather than color.

Consistency rule: For every species in your analysis, verify that you are using a TP male image. If a TP male image is not available for a given species, it is better to exclude that species than to use an IP image and introduce noise.

Promising Questions for This Clade

  1. Sexual System and Color Complexity — Scarinae is the ideal clade for testing whether protogynous hermaphroditism drives the evolution of extreme dichromatism. Compare TP male conspicuousness across species and ask whether the magnitude of IP–TP color difference correlates with mating system intensity (haremic vs. group spawning).

  2. Body Size and Color Complexity — With a 10-fold size range from Cryptotomus to Bolbometopon, parrotfishes offer a strong gradient for testing whether larger species can “afford” more complex color patterns (greater visual target area, reduced predation risk).

  3. Biogeographic Color Comparison — The Atlantic (Sparisoma) vs. Indo-Pacific (Scarus, Chlorurus) split is one of the cleanest biogeographic contrasts available. Do Indo-Pacific parrotfishes occupy a broader region of color space? Has the Atlantic lineage been more constrained in its color evolution?

  4. Diet and Conspicuousness — Scrapers vs. excavators differ in feeding posture and reef-surface exposure time. Do excavators (which spend more time head-down on the reef face) differ in conspicuousness from scrapers?
