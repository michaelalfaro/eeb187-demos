Halichoerine — Coris-Allies and Halichoeres Wrasses

Natural History

The Halichoerine clade is the largest group in the course, encompassing approximately 140 species drawn from some of the most species-rich genera within Labridae: Halichoeres (~83 species), Coris (~19 species), Thalassoma (~17 species), Macropharyngodon (~10 species), Anampses (~11 species), and several smaller genera. This clade represents the julidine lineage of wrasses — small to medium-sized, typically elongate reef fishes that are among the most abundant and conspicuous members of tropical reef communities worldwide. The phylogenomic analysis of Brownstein et al. (2025) confirmed that multiple lineages within this clade diversified rapidly during the Miocene, contributing to the explosive radiation of labrid diversity on coral reefs.

Halichoeres is the single largest wrasse genus and is circumtropically distributed across the Atlantic, Eastern Pacific, and Indo-Pacific. Molecular phylogenetics has revealed that Halichoeres as traditionally defined is likely polyphyletic — species currently placed in the genus may not all share a single common ancestor exclusive of other genera (Barber & Bellwood, 2005; Price et al., 2018). This means that patterns of color evolution mapped onto the Halichoeres phylogeny may reflect deep evolutionary divergences rather than recent radiation within a single lineage. The polyphyly of Halichoeres is itself an important finding for your analysis: it means that apparent “within-genus” color diversity may actually span multiple independent evolutionary lineages.

A defining biogeographic story in this clade involves geminate species pairs across the Isthmus of Panama. When the isthmus closed approximately 3 million years ago, it divided ancestral populations of marine species into Caribbean (Atlantic) and Eastern Pacific descendants. Halichoeres contains several well-documented geminate pairs: H. bivittatus (Atlantic) and H. dispilus (Pacific), H. maculipinna (Atlantic) and H. chierchiae (Pacific). Price et al. (2018) showed that five of 25 speciation events in New World Halichoeres occurred across this barrier, though not all at the time of final closure — some trans-Isthmian splits are much older (up to ~20 Ma). These geminate pairs offer a natural experiment: how much has color diverged between sister species separated by a well-dated vicariance event?

Ecologically, halichoerine wrasses are primarily invertivores, foraging on sand flats, rubble zones, and reef edges for small crustaceans, polychaetes, and mollusks. Several genera have distinctive foraging behaviors: Coris species dig through sand for buried invertebrates; Thalassoma species are fast-swimming generalists that also pick zooplankton from the water column; Macropharyngodon (“leopard wrasses”) specialize on small, hard-shelled invertebrates (foraminifera, ostracods) and have distinctive pharyngeal jaw dentition. One of the most widespread behaviors in the clade is sand-sleeping: many species bury themselves in sand at night or when threatened, a behavior that may impose constraints on coloration (sand-sleeping species may benefit from cryptic dorsal coloration visible from above).

Color Pattern Diversity

The halichoerine clade contains some of the most extreme color diversity in any single labrid lineage. Macropharyngodon leopard wrasses display intricate, almost psychedelic patterns of teal spots, orange rosettes, and irregular black outlines on a pale background — each individual has a unique pattern, like a fingerprint. Thalassoma species are characterized by bold facial stripes and head patterns: T. lunare (moon wrasse) has a green body with vivid magenta-to-yellow face markings, while T. bifasciatum (bluehead wrasse) is one of the most studied reef fishes in the world, with TP males displaying a brilliant blue head separated from a green body by black-and-white vertical bars. Coris gaimard undergoes dramatic ontogenetic color change: juveniles are bright orange-red with white-edged black dorsal spots (“clown” pattern), while adults develop a dark blue-green body with brilliant blue facial spots.

Halichoeres species span the full spectrum from drab to vivid. Some sand-associated species (e.g., H. poeyi, H. bivittatus IP) are olive-green or brown with subtle stripes and blotches, while others (e.g., H. ornatissimus, the ornate wrasse) display rainbow-like patterns of red, green, blue, and orange facial stripes. This genus-level color variation — from cryptic to maximally conspicuous — within a single (albeit polyphyletic) genus makes Halichoeres ideal for testing ecological predictors of color evolution.

Anampses wrasses show a different pattern: many species display intricate spotting and line patterns on a dark body, with dramatic IP/TP differences. A. meleagrides females are black with white spots, while TP males are dark green with blue facial lines and an orange tail.

Dimorphism and Ontogeny Warnings

Data quality: what to watch for

IP/TP dimorphism — present and important. Like all labrid clades, halichoerine wrasses are protogynous hermaphrodites with IP/TP color differences. The magnitude of dimorphism varies by genus:

  • Thalassoma: Strong dimorphism. TP males (e.g., bluehead wrasse blue phase) are dramatically different from IPs (yellow/brown). Use TP males.
  • Halichoeres: Moderate to strong dimorphism. TP males typically show more vivid colors and bolder markings than IPs. Use TP males.
  • Coris: Moderate dimorphism. TP males are generally more intensely colored with more defined facial patterns.
  • Macropharyngodon: Moderate dimorphism. Both phases are intricately patterned, but TP males tend toward more saturated colors and sharper pattern boundaries.
  • Anampses: Strong dimorphism. IP and TP can look like completely different species. Use TP males.

General rule: Use TP male images consistently. In labrids, females and juveniles often retain the juvenile color morph. If you cannot determine sex, choose the most vivid/boldly patterned individual, which is likely a TP male.

Ontogenetic changes: Coris species undergo dramatic juvenile-to-adult color transformation (e.g., C. gaimard juvenile “clown” pattern to adult blue-green). Thalassoma juveniles are typically uniformly colored (yellow, green, or brown) and lack the facial markings of adults. Exclude juveniles.

Dataset size management: With 140 species, this is by far the largest student dataset. Not all species will have high-quality photographs. Rather than including poor-quality or uncertain images for every species, focus on a well-sampled subset of 50–70 species that have:

  • Clear, high-resolution lateral-view photographs
  • Confirmed TP male individuals
  • Verified species identification

A well-curated subset of 60 species will produce more reliable pavo metrics than a noisy dataset of 140 species with mixed phases, poor images, and misidentifications. Document which species you excluded and why — this is good scientific practice, not a weakness.

Polyphyly of Halichoeres: Your tree may show Halichoeres species scattered across multiple parts of the phylogeny rather than forming a single clade. This is not a tree error — it reflects the genuine polyphyly of the genus. When reporting results, consider analyzing well-supported subclades of Halichoeres separately rather than treating the genus as a single unit.

Promising Questions for This Clade

  1. Biogeographic Color Comparison — The geminate species pairs across the Isthmus of Panama provide a powerful natural experiment. Have Caribbean and Pacific sister species diverged in color metrics since vicariant separation? Is the magnitude of color divergence correlated with time since divergence?

  2. Cross-Family Comparison — As the largest labrid clade in the course, halichoerine wrasses contribute the most species to the combined labrid mega-analysis. Compare the color morphospace occupied by your clade to the other three labrid groups (Scarinae, Cirrhilabrini, Hypsigenyinae). Does the largest clade occupy the broadest color space?

  3. Phylogenetic Signal — With 140 species (or your curated subset), you have the best statistical power for estimating phylogenetic signal (Blomberg’s K, Pagel’s lambda). Is color more phylogenetically conserved in some genera (Macropharyngodon, with its distinctive spotted pattern) than others (Halichoeres, with its broad color range)?

  4. Axis of Diversification — With extreme color diversity spanning from cryptic sand-dwellers to psychedelic leopard wrasses, which axis of pavo color space (brightness, saturation, hue, pattern complexity) shows the greatest disparity? Is the primary axis of color diversification the same across all genera?

  5. Swimming Mode and Pattern — Halichoerine wrasses include both labriform swimmers (pectoral fin propulsion) and more body-undulatory swimmers. Does swimming mode correlate with stripe orientation or color pattern geometry?
