Taxonomy and Phylogenetics

The Halichoerinae (or “julidine” lineage sensu Westneat & Alfaro, 2005) is a large, monophyletic radiation within Labridae that includes the genera Halichoeres, Coris, Thalassoma, Macropharyngodon, Anampses, Pseudocheilinus, Pseudojuloides, Stethojulis, and several smaller allies. Westneat and Alfaro’s (2005) molecular phylogeny based on mitochondrial 12S, 16S, and nuclear tmo-4C4 sequences recovered this group as a well-supported clade nested within the broader labrid tree, sister to the cheiline + scarine clade. More recent phylogenomic work has both confirmed the monophyly of the broad julidine lineage and refined intergeneric relationships within it (Cowman & Bellwood, 2011; Hodge & Bellwood, 2016).

The most taxonomically important finding to keep in mind when interpreting your tree is that Halichoeres as currently circumscribed is not monophyletic. Barber and Bellwood (2005) used mitochondrial cytochrome b* and 16S to show that Atlantic, Eastern Pacific, and Indo-Pacific Halichoeres do not form a single exclusive clade; instead, several lineages are more closely related to other genera (e.g., Macropharyngodon, Anampses) than to other Halichoeres. Rocha et al. (2010) reinforced this conclusion for the New World species, recovering at least two independent Halichoeres radiations on either side of the Isthmus of Panama. For your analyses, this means that any “within-Halichoeres” comparison actually spans multiple deep evolutionary lineages, and apparent color disparity within the genus reflects ancient divergences, not a recent burst within a single tight clade.

Diversification rates within the halichoerine clade accelerated during the Miocene, coinciding with expansion of Indo-Australian Archipelago (IAA) reef habitat (Cowman & Bellwood, 2011; Hodge & Bellwood, 2016). Repeated dispersal out of the IAA “biodiversity hotspot” appears to have seeded peripheral reef regions, including the Caribbean, Eastern Pacific, and Hawaiian provinces. The result is a clade whose geographic structure is layered: deep biogeographic signal at the genus level is overlain by recent, often shallow species-level divergences across well-dated barriers.

Natural History

Halichoerine wrasses are small to medium-sized (typically 8–40 cm TL), elongate reef fishes that swim by labriform pectoral-fin propulsion, a locomotor mode shared across Labridae and tied to maneuverable foraging in structurally complex habitat (Westneat & Alfaro, 2005). They are among the most conspicuous diurnal predators on tropical reefs worldwide. Most species are benthic invertivores, feeding on small crustaceans, polychaetes, gastropods, and ophiuroids picked from rubble, sand, or live coral interstices. Coris species are specialized “sand-flippers,” using strong pharyngeal jaws to crush hard-shelled prey extracted by digging; Macropharyngodon leopard wrasses target small foraminiferans and ostracods; Thalassoma and Pseudocheilinus are more generalized water-column foragers that also take zooplankton.

A widespread behavior in the clade is sand-sleeping: many halichoerine species (essentially all Halichoeres, Coris, Anampses, Macropharyngodon, Pseudojuloides) bury themselves in sand at dusk or when alarmed. This habit constrains body shape (laterally compressed, elongate) and may exert selection on dorsal coloration visible to aerial or planktonic predators during the brief moments fish are exposed at the substrate surface.

A second defining ecological feature is biogeographic structure. The closure of the Central American Seaway ~3.1 Ma (Coates & Stallard, 2013) produced multiple geminate species pairs within New World Halichoeres (e.g., H. bivittatus/H. dispilus, H. maculipinna/H. chierchiae). Rocha et al. (2010), however, demonstrated that not all trans-Isthmian splits date to final closure; several are substantially older (up to ~20 Ma), reflecting earlier episodes of restriction. The clade therefore offers replicate natural experiments at varying time depths — an unusually powerful setting for asking how rapidly color and pattern diverge after vicariance.

Visual Ecology and Signaling

Wrasses possess among the most spectrally rich visual systems documented in teleosts. They retain functional copies of all four ancestral cone opsin classes (SWS1 UV-sensitive; SWS2 violet/blue; RH2 blue-green; LWS red), and several lineages have undergone tandem duplication of the RH2 and SWS2 genes (Cortesi et al., 2015; Musilová et al., 2021). Cortesi et al. (2015) showed in a comparative survey across damselfishes and wrasses that opsin gene expression is dynamically tuned during ontogeny and across photic environments: juveniles and shallow-water species typically express UV- and short-wavelength-sensitive opsins more strongly, while deeper-living individuals shift expression toward longer wavelengths. Stieb et al. (2023) demonstrated that this expression plasticity persists in adults and can shift on weeks-to-months timescales when ambient spectral conditions change.

Functionally, this visual repertoire means halichoerine wrasses can both produce and perceive signals in the UV and short-blue regions of the spectrum, where many of their conspecific facial patterns reflect strongly (Cortesi et al., 2016; Marshall et al., 2003 in the broader reef-fish context). UV-reflective face markings in Thalassoma and Halichoeres TP males, and the iridescent blue lines on Anampses and Macropharyngodon, are therefore not merely human-visible ornaments but private or semi-private channels visible to conspecifics under reef light. Because pavo’s sensmodel and vismodel workflows let you reweight reflectance spectra against any visual model, this clade is a strong target for asking whether IP/TP differences predicted to be “subtle” in human RGB are in fact dramatic under wrasse-relevant cone weightings.

Color Pattern Diversity

The halichoerine clade contains some of the broadest color disparity in any single labrid lineage. Macropharyngodon leopard wrasses display intricate reticulated patterns of teal, orange, and black rosettes against pale backgrounds, with each individual carrying an effectively unique pattern. Thalassoma species are characterized by bold facial bars and head shields: T. bifasciatum (bluehead wrasse) TP males display a brilliant blue head separated from a green body by black-and-white vertical bars and are among the most studied reef fishes globally (Warner & Swearer, 1991). T. lunare exhibits a green body with vivid magenta-to-yellow facial markings. Coris gaimard shows pronounced ontogenetic color change: juveniles are vermillion-orange with white-edged black dorsal spots, while adults develop a dark blue-green body with brilliant blue facial spots.

Halichoeres spans nearly the full possible range from cryptic to maximally conspicuous. Some sand-associated species (e.g., H. poeyi, H. bivittatus IP) are olive-green or brown with subtle stripes, while others (e.g., H. ornatissimus, H. iridis) display rainbow-like sequences of red, green, blue, and orange facial lines. Anampses wrasses present a different syndrome: intricate white-spotting or fine-line patterns on dark bodies, with extreme IP/TP divergence (A. meleagrides females are black with white spots, TP males are dark green with blue facial lines and an orange tail). Hodge et al. (2020a) showed in a phylogenetic comparative framework that pattern complexity and chromatic contrast vary independently across reef fishes, and the halichoerine clade is an excellent within-family example: Macropharyngodon is high-complexity, moderate-contrast; Thalassoma is low-complexity, high-contrast; Halichoeres spans both axes.

Sexual Dimorphism, Ontogeny, and Polymorphism

All halichoerine wrasses studied to date are protogynous hermaphrodites: individuals mature as females (Initial Phase, IP) and a subset later transition to functional males (Terminal Phase, TP) following sex change, typically triggered by loss of a dominant male from the social group (Warner & Robertson, 1978; Warner et al., 1985). In many species, IP individuals include both functional females and small “sneaker” males that retain female-like coloration — a third phenotypic class that is genetically male but visually indistinguishable from females (Warner & Swearer, 1991). Terminal Phase males are larger, brighter, and behave as territorial spawners.

The magnitude of IP/TP color divergence varies systematically across the clade:

  • Thalassoma: extreme. TP males (e.g., bluehead wrasse blue phase) are essentially unrecognizable as the same species as IP individuals (Warner & Swearer, 1991).
  • Halichoeres: moderate to strong. TP males show more saturated colors, more contrasting facial bands, and often a unique flank spot or bar absent in IPs.
  • Coris: moderate. TP males have sharper facial pattern boundaries and brighter blues.
  • Macropharyngodon: moderate. Both phases are intricately patterned, but TP males have more saturated reds/oranges.
  • Anampses: extreme. IP and TP look like different species.

Ontogenetic color change is layered on top of this dichromatism. Coris species undergo striking juvenile-to-adult transformations (the C. gaimard “clown” juvenile becoming an adult “yellowtail coris”). Thalassoma juveniles are typically uniformly colored and lack adult facial markings. These ontogenetic transitions reflect changing visual ecology — juveniles often occupy different microhabitats than adults and may experience different predator regimes (Cortesi et al., 2016).

IP/TP Dichromatism — A Clade Deep Dive

Because IP/TP dichromatism is the single most important source of within-species color variation in this clade, it requires careful treatment in any color-evolution analysis. The phenomenon arises from the intersection of three biological systems:

1. Socially controlled sex change. Warner and Robertson (1978), in a foundational study of Caribbean labrids, showed that protogynous sex change is triggered by social cues — specifically, the disappearance of dominant males allows the largest female in a group to transition. Warner et al. (1985) and Warner and Swearer (1991) extended this to Thalassoma bifasciatum, demonstrating that sex change is fast (days to weeks), involves complete gonadal reorganization, and produces a behaviorally and chromatically distinct individual. The size-advantage model (Warner, 1975) predicts protogyny will evolve when male reproductive success increases steeply with size and when small individuals have higher female fitness — conditions met in most halichoerine mating systems.

2. Alternative reproductive tactics. In many halichoerine species, including T. bifasciatum and several Halichoeres, small males in IP coloration (“sneakers” or “streakers”) participate in group spawnings dominated by TP males (Warner & Swearer, 1991; Kuwamura et al., 2002). This means IP coloration is not strictly diagnostic of female sex — a fraction of IP individuals are male. For pavo analyses, this caveat is conceptually important: an “IP” photograph may represent a female or a sneaker male, and these may not differ chromatically.

3. Hormonal control of pigment cells. The TP transition involves coordinated changes in xanthophore, erythrophore, iridophore, and melanophore distribution under the control of gonadal steroids (Warner et al., 2006). Iridophore density increases in the head region of TP males, producing the characteristic structural blues and greens of Thalassoma, Halichoeres, and Anampses face patterns. Because iridophore-based colors are angle-dependent, photographic standardization (consistent lateral view, diffuse lighting) is essential.

Practical implication for color-evolution analyses. If you mix IPs and TPs within a species, you will inflate within-species variance and deflate between-species effect sizes. The correct strategy is to choose one phase consistently across all species (TP males are conventional, because they are the more conspicuous “advertised” phenotype) and document any species for which only the alternative phase was available. For questions specifically about dichromatism itself (e.g., “Is the magnitude of TP-vs-IP divergence correlated with mating-system intensity?”), you should sample both phases for each species and compute a per-species dichromatism index.

Data Quality Warnings

What to watch for in image selection

IP/TP dimorphism — present and important. Use TP male images consistently. If you cannot determine sex from a photograph, choose the most vivid, boldly patterned individual — in halichoerines this is almost certainly a TP male. Sneaker males are visually indistinguishable from females and need not be excluded separately from IPs.

Ontogenetic color change. Coris species (especially C. gaimard, C. aygula, C. julis) undergo dramatic juvenile-to-adult transformations. Thalassoma juveniles are typically plain-colored. Exclude obvious juveniles.

Polyphyly of Halichoeres. Your tree may show Halichoeres species scattered across multiple parts of the phylogeny (Barber & Bellwood, 2005; Rocha et al., 2010). This is real biology, not a tree error. Consider analyzing well-supported subclades separately rather than treating the genus as a single unit.

Iridophore-based colors are angle-dependent. Structural blues and greens on TP male faces will change apparent hue with lighting geometry. Prefer photographs taken in diffuse natural light with a clean lateral view. Avoid strobed images that exaggerate iridescence.

Dataset size management. With 140 species, this is the largest student dataset. Not all species will have high-quality photographs. Curate a well-sampled subset of 50–70 species with verified TP male identification rather than including poor or uncertain images for every species. Document exclusions explicitly — this is good scientific practice.

Sand-sleeping species. Several species (Halichoeres, Anampses, Macropharyngodon) may be photographed half-buried or with disturbed dorsal pigmentation. Exclude these and use clean, fully exposed individuals.

Promising Questions for This Clade

  1. Biogeographic Color Comparison — The Isthmus of Panama geminate species pairs provide a well-dated natural experiment. Have Caribbean and Pacific sister species diverged in pavo color metrics since vicariant separation? Is divergence magnitude correlated with time since splitting? Note that Rocha et al. (2010) showed not all trans-Isthmian splits date to ~3 Ma — you can leverage this variation in divergence times.

  2. Cross-Family Comparison — As the largest labrid clade in the course, halichoerine wrasses contribute the most species to the combined labrid mega-analysis. Compare the color morphospace occupied by your clade to Scarinae, Cirrhilabrini, and Hypsigenyinae. Does the largest clade occupy the broadest color space, or is disparity decoupled from species richness?

  3. Phylogenetic Signal — With 140 species (or your curated subset of ~60), you have strong statistical power for Blomberg’s K and Pagel’s lambda. Is color more phylogenetically conserved in Macropharyngodon (distinctive spotted pattern) than in Halichoeres (highly variable)?

  4. Axis of Diversification — With color spanning cryptic sand-dwellers to psychedelic leopard wrasses, which axis of pavo color space (brightness, saturation, hue, pattern complexity) shows greatest disparity? Hodge et al. (2020a) show pattern complexity and chromatic contrast vary independently across reef fishes — test this within your clade.

  5. Swimming Mode and Pattern — Halichoerine wrasses are predominantly labriform swimmers but vary in body elongation and tail shape. Does fin-and-body morphology correlate with stripe orientation or pattern geometry?

  6. Sexual Dichromatism Index — For species where both IP and TP photographs are available, compute a per-species dichromatism index (e.g., Euclidean distance in pavo color space between IP and TP). Does dichromatism correlate with mating-system intensity (group spawning vs pair spawning; estimated from literature)?
